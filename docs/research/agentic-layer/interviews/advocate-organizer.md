# Interview: advocate-organizer (Dana Okafor, composite persona)

**Run date:** 2026-07-23
**Stimulus:** `02-layer-inventory.md` (contract v1.16), plus reported
live-test observations from 2026-07-23 (guidance-file caveat carriage,
guidance-file-only misattribution, no changelog/diff capability, maintainer's
own FOIA-via-assistant workflow)
**Interviewer:** Lead Researcher (LR)
**Participant:** Dana Okafor — volunteer chair, a Northwest Side
neighborhood bike/walk advocacy group (composite persona, grounded in
`chi-pro-advocate.md` and `chi-community-organizer.md`)

## Transcript

### 1. Context

**LR:** Tell me about your role, and walk me through the last time you tried
to influence a street-safety decision. What did that actually involve, step
by step?

**Dana:** I chair a volunteer group — maybe fifteen of us who actually show
up, a mailing list of a couple hundred. Day job, this is not it; I do this
nights and weekends. Last real push was six weeks ago — CDOT opened public
comment on a Neighborhood Bike Network corridor that runs through our area,
three-week window, and we wanted enough of our people to submit comments
that it registered as more than the four cranks who always show up against
paint on the street.

**LR:** What did "running that" actually look like day to day?

**Dana:** First thing was just figuring out the window was open at all —
that came from a coalition email, somebody at Active Trans forwarded the
CDOT notice to a listserv I'm on, not from CDOT reaching me directly. Then I
had maybe two days before our next meeting to turn that into something I
could put in front of fifteen volunteers and say "here's what we're asking
for and here's the deadline." I drafted a one-pager — corridor description,
what the project actually proposed, three bullet points on why we support
it, a crash number I remembered from an old ATA ward letter that I had to
go dig up because I didn't trust my memory of it, and a link to the comment
form with the deadline in bold at the top.

**LR:** Who do you need to convince, and what does convinced look like for
them?

**Dana:** Two different audiences, honestly. CDOT's comment form itself —
they say they read every comment, I have no way to verify that, but volume
and specificity supposedly move the needle. And then separately, our alder,
because even a CDOT-favorable project can get killed or watered down by
aldermanic prerogative if the alder doesn't want it — I watched that happen
to a totally different project two years ago. Convinced, for the alder,
means showing up at ward night with enough people and enough specifics that
saying no costs him something politically.

### 2. Current practice & workarounds

**LR:** Where did the crash number in that one-pager actually come from?

**Dana:** That's the embarrassing part. I didn't have a current source. I
remembered Active Trans citing a number for this corridor in a ward letter
from maybe a year and a half ago, so I emailed a contact there and asked her
to just tell me the number again because I didn't trust myself to go pull it
correctly from the city portal under a two-day deadline. She got back to me
the next morning with a number and I used it. If she hadn't answered fast, I
genuinely don't know what I would have put in that slot — probably nothing,
or something vaguer like "this corridor has seen serious crashes," which
reads weaker.

**LR:** Has that happened before — needing a number on a deadline and not
having a reliable way to get it yourself?

**Dana:** Constantly. It's the single most common failure in how we operate.
We are not data people. I have one volunteer who's good with spreadsheets
and he's also got a toddler and a full-time job, so he's not always
reachable on my timeline. Most of what we cite, we cite because somebody
else — Active Trans, a news article, a city press release — already said it
in a form we could copy. We almost never go to a raw source ourselves. It's
too slow and I don't fully trust myself not to mess it up.

**LR:** Tell me about a time missing data, or a number you couldn't get in
time, actually cost you something in an argument.

**Dana:** The 18th Street thing — not our corridor, but I use it as a
cautionary tale internally. Protected lane got installed, then the alder had
it ripped back out later, and from what I understand, part of what let that
happen was that nobody had a clean, current, defensible "here's what this
lane is actually doing" packet ready the moment it started getting
challenged — it was reactive instead of ready. I don't want that to be us.
Right now if somebody challenged our corridor's safety case in a meeting
next week, I don't have anything current sitting ready. I'd have to go
build it from scratch again, on whatever the deadline turns out to be.

**LR:** What do you distrust, when it comes to street-safety data, and what
earned that?

**Dana:** 311 complaint data, for our neighborhood specifically. I've had
this argument in a room — somebody points at a 311 heat map and says "see,
nobody's complaining here, it's fine," and that's backwards, that's a
reporting gap, not a safety fact, and I have to explain that every single
time because the map doesn't say it about itself. And anything that's
citywide-only when the fight is ward-by-ward — a statistic that's true for
Chicago overall doesn't move anybody at a ward night, they want their
corridor, their intersection.

**LR:** You mentioned an assistant hasn't come up yet — do you use one for
this work at all?

**Dana:** Every week, honestly, just not for the number itself, historically
— for the writing. I draft the one-pager skeleton, then I run it through an
assistant to tighten the language, sometimes to draft the comment-form
submission text volunteers can personalize instead of starting from a blank
box, sometimes to draft the follow-up "here's what happened, thanks for
showing up" email after a ward night. It's saved me real hours on the
writing side. I just haven't trusted it with the numbers going into that
writing — that part's still me, or Active Trans, or whoever picked up the
phone.

**LR:** Why the writing and not the numbers?

**Dana:** Because if it writes an awkward sentence, I catch that reading it
back — bad writing is obvious to me. If it hands me a wrong crash count, I
have no idea unless I already happen to know the right one, and by the time
I'd find out, it's already gone out to two hundred people or worse, said out
loud at ward night. The failure mode for language is embarrassing. The
failure mode for a number is the thing that gets us laughed out of the room
or, worse, believed and wrong.

### 3. Stimulus walkthrough

**LR:** Let me describe what's actually published, and then I want to talk
through specific things an assistant could *do* for a group like yours, not
just answer questions. There's a site that publishes ward rankings, crash
trends, infrastructure stats per corridor, a hand-curated list of proposed
CDOT projects with dated, volunteer-reviewed status, local news coverage
matched to specific projects and wards, and City Council activity — sponsor
records, hearings. All of it published as files an assistant can read
directly, plus a guidance file that tells the assistant how to talk about
the caveats. Live tests this week found assistants that read that guidance
file did carry the caveats correctly into their answers, including
redirecting an obstruction-data question to the right place instead of
guessing.

**Dana:** Okay — that's already more than what I have. If I could ask my
assistant "what's the current crash count on this corridor, sourced" and
trust the answer enough to put it directly in a one-pager without a
phone call to Active Trans, that alone would have saved me the anxious
two-day wait I described. What would I need to check first — is there a
published methodology page I could point a skeptical volunteer to if they
asked "where'd this come from"?

**LR:** Yes — every number carries a human-readable page link and a tier
label for how trustworthy that category of data is. Here's the failure mode
the same tests found, though: one assistant that read only the guidance
file — not the actual ward data file — produced a confident wrong ward
number, with the caveats attached correctly around it.

**Dana:** [pause] That's the nightmare version of what I already do
manually, just automated. Right now when I get a number wrong, it's because
I misremembered something or a source was stale — that's on me, and I can
usually trace it. If the assistant hands me something confidently wrong with
the caveat sitting right next to it looking legitimate, I don't have a
process for catching that, because I'm not the one who'd know to
double-check it — the whole reason I'd be asking it is that I don't already
know the answer.

**LR:** What would you actually do with that risk, practically?

**Dana:** Cross-check anything that's going in front of more than my own
volunteers — anything hitting the comment form, ward night, or a public
email — against the actual published page myself, at least the headline
number, before it goes out. Which cuts into the time savings, but it doesn't
erase it, because drafting the whole packet is still faster even with one
manual check at the end than building the whole thing from nothing.

**LR:** Let's talk functions now, not just answers. Say your assistant could
*do* ongoing work with this data, not just respond once. First: it could
watch your corridor and your ward on a schedule and tell you what changed —
crash counts updated, a project's status changed, new news coverage
appeared. Would that change anything for your group?

**Dana:** That might be the single biggest one, actually, more than the
number-lookup. Here's why: I found out about our comment window from a
forwarded email, two days before I needed to act, from someone else's
listserv — not because I was watching for it. If something had been
checking our corridor weekly and told me "comment period opened, three
weeks left" the day it opened, I'd have had three weeks to build that
packet properly instead of two panicked days. That's not a nicer version of
what I do — that's a different amount of time to work with.

**LR:** What would you need to trust that monitoring — what would make you
turn it off?

**Dana:** If it missed the thing that mattered once — if a real deadline
came and went and it didn't flag it because of some data quirk — I'd stop
trusting it silently in the background and go back to checking manually,
because a monitoring system that fails silently is worse than no monitoring
system, I'd have false confidence I was covered. I'd want to know, somehow,
that it actually ran that week, not just trust that it would have told me
if something happened.

**LR:** Second function: document production — drafting a ward one-pager, a
public-comment submission, or an alder email, pulling the current numbers in
directly rather than you copying them by hand.

**Dana:** That's the writing half of what I already do, plus the number half
I currently can't do myself. If it drafts the whole one-pager — corridor,
current numbers, sourced, status of the project, three bullets on the ask —
and I'm reviewing instead of building from a blank page, that's real. I'd
still read every word before it goes out, and I'd still want the number
part specifically flagged somehow as "here's exactly where this came from"
so I can spot-check just that piece fast instead of re-verifying the whole
document.

**LR:** Would you let it send anything on its own — the follow-up email to
your list, say, without you reading it first?

**Dana:** No. Not that, not the comment-form submission, not anything with
my group's name on it or that goes to CDOT or the alder's office. I'll let
it draft, I will not let it send. That's not really about the data being
wrong, even — it's about tone and judgment, whether it sounds like us,
whether it's saying something politically dumb I'd catch and it wouldn't
know to. I make that call for every volunteer's draft too, honestly, not
just an assistant's.

**LR:** Third: multi-step investigation — asking something like "why does
this ward rank worse than the neighboring one, what's actually driving it,"
and having it chase that down across corridors, crash trends, and project
status rather than you doing that legwork yourself.

**Dana:** Useful for prep, less useful for the ask itself. If I'm walking
into ward night and someone's going to challenge "why us, why this corridor
and not that one," having a real answer instead of "it just feels worse
here" would matter — that's exactly the kind of question that ends an
argument or loses it depending on whether I have a real answer. I'd use
that the night before, not live in the room — I wouldn't trust myself to
verify a live multi-step answer on the spot in front of people.

**LR:** Fourth thing, and I want your honest reaction, not a hypothetical
one: the person who maintains this whole site runs its FOIA requests through
an assistant — drafting the letters, tracking the statutory deadlines,
booking the acknowledgment follow-ups.

**Dana:** Huh. That's — okay, that's actually the thing that would change
capacity the most for us, and it's not really about bike data specifically.
We've never filed a FOIA as a group, not once, and it's not because we
never needed to — it's because none of us knows the deadline rules or how
to word one so it doesn't get bounced back "overly broad." If drafting one
and tracking the clock is a thing an assistant reliably does, that's a
door that's currently just closed to us, not slow for us. That's different
from the other three — those save me time on things I already do. This is
something I currently don't do at all.

### 4. Gap probing

**LR:** Of the four — monitoring, drafting, investigation, FOIA — which
would you actually adopt first, for real, not which sounds best?

**Dana:** Monitoring. Because it doesn't require me to change anything
about how I already work, it just closes the "we found out too late" gap,
which is the thing that's bitten us the most times. Drafting is close
behind, but I already have a version of drafting — this would just make it
better and add sourced numbers. Monitoring is the one where I currently
have nothing, not even a bad manual version.

**LR:** What's a missed CDOT feedback window actually cost your group,
concretely, when it happens?

**Dana:** We lose the chance to be on record before the decision's locked
in — and unlike a hearing where you can sometimes still show up and speak,
a written comment window that's closed is just closed. Then all that's left
is showing up at ward night after the fact and either accepting what got
decided or fighting an uphill battle to reopen something that already has
momentum. It's the difference between shaping a thing and objecting to a
thing, and objecting always plays worse in the room.

**LR:** There's currently no way to see "what changed since last week" on
this site — no changelog, no diff, no alert. An assistant that wanted to
monitor your corridor would have to remember last week's numbers itself and
compare. Does that change how useful the monitoring idea is to you?

**Dana:** It means it's not a thing that exists today, just a thing that
could — which, fine, I figured as much, nothing about my week has an alert
built in for anything. What worries me more is the flip side: if it's
storing "last week's numbers" itself to compare, and it gets that stored
copy wrong somehow, does it tell me something changed that didn't, or miss
something that did? I don't know enough to answer that myself, that's a
question for whoever builds it, not something I can evaluate from a chair
in a church basement.

**LR:** The site explicitly doesn't normalize by ridership because it has no
volume data. Does that change how you'd use any of this?

**Dana:** Not really, honestly, because I already know to say "raw count,
not per-rider" out loud if someone challenges it in a room — that's a
familiar caveat, not a new one, whether a human or an assistant hands it to
me. What I'd actually want as "good enough" exposure data is something
dumb and simple, honestly — even just "this street has a bike lane and gets
used, versus this one doesn't" would help more than nothing, I don't need
precision, I need something better than silence on the question.

### 5. Magic-wand close

**LR:** Magic wand — one dataset that doesn't publicly exist appears, clean
and current. What is it?

**Dana:** A single feed of every open public-comment window for every CDOT
project citywide, with deadlines, in one place, that doesn't depend on
somebody forwarding me an email from a listserv I happen to be on. That's
the thing that's actually failed me, repeatedly — not the crash numbers,
the *timing*. I've built my entire process around finding out fast enough
by luck.

**LR:** One chart or artifact you could put in front of your hardest
audience — the alder, say — that ends an argument.

**Dana:** A one-page corridor packet: current sourced crash number, what
the project actually proposes, and — this is the part I don't have today —
how many of my volunteers submitted a comment, timestamped, so it's not
"some people care" but "here are forty-one specific comments, here's when
they came in." Numbers plus turnout plus dates, one page, nothing he can
wave off as vague.

**LR:** What would make you stop using something like this after trying it
once?

**Dana:** One wrong number that goes out under our name and gets caught by
someone hostile to us in the room — that's it, same as any source I use.
And separately: if the monitoring silently missed a real deadline once,
I'm done trusting it to watch anything for me again, I'd go back to hoping
somebody forwards me the email in time, which I already know is a bad
system, but at least it's a bad system I understand.
