# Interview transcript — Renata Okafor (simulated)

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time. Can you tell me about your role —
not a job title, just: when do you end up needing information about street
safety where you live?

**Renata:** I'm in Logan Square, I do remote customer support, and I ride a
bike sometimes — not everywhere, just three or four routes I've had
memorized for years. Milwaukee down to the blue line stop, a loop around
the park on Sundays when it's quiet, that kind of thing. I have retinitis
pigmentosa, so my vision's been narrowing for about a decade, and at this
point I don't drive and I don't really "look things up" the way a sighted
person does — I ask, out loud usually, and something reads me an answer.
That's just how I get information now, not just street stuff, everything.
The last time street safety actually came up was maybe six weeks ago — a
building near my usual cut-through on Kedzie put up construction fencing,
and I needed to know if the bike lane was still passable or if I needed a
different way to my Sunday loop.

**Interviewer:** Walk me through that, step by step. What did you actually
do?

**Renata:** I asked my phone — I use an assistant, I'd rather not say the
brand, but it's one of the big conversational ones, I typed into it because
typing is honestly faster for me than getting into a voice back-and-forth
in a noisy kitchen. I asked something like "is the Kedzie bike lane near
Logan Square closed for construction." It gave me a paragraph — a
confident-sounding paragraph — that said there was a permit for scaffolding
on that block and cyclists should expect a detour. It didn't cite anything
I could check, no source I recognized. I read it, I believed it enough to
change my route that day, and I found out later from a neighbor that the
lane was actually fine, the fencing was on the sidewalk side only. So I
rode the "wrong" way for about two weeks based on nothing.

**Interviewer:** How did you find out from the neighbor — was that a
correction, or did it just come up?

**Renata:** It came up. I mentioned I'd been going the long way because of
construction and she said "what construction, I biked past there
yesterday." That's usually how I find out I got bad information — someone
sighted casually contradicts it in conversation. I don't have a way to go
verify it myself. I can't pull up a map and squint at it, and even if I
could see better, most of these map sites aren't built for a screen reader
— they're a canvas element, my screen reader announces "graphic" and stops
there. So my checking mechanism is other people, basically. Which is slow
and depends on who I happen to talk to that week.

**Interviewer:** You said you don't have a way to verify it yourself —
has that come up in other contexts, not just the one story?

**Renata:** All the time. Not just bike stuff — I'll ask about a CTA
reroute, or whether a park district pool is open, and I get an answer that
sounds complete, and I genuinely cannot tell if it's current or if the
assistant found some outdated forum post from three years ago and treated
it like today's information. Sighted people can eyeball a webpage and go
"oh this article's from 2019," the date's right there. I don't get that
signal for free. I have to specifically ask "when was this published" and
half the time the assistant can't actually tell me, it just restates the
claim more confidently.

**Interviewer:** What have you built for yourself — habits, workarounds,
people you check with — because nothing gives you a reliable, checkable
answer directly?

**Renata:** A few things. One, I keep a mental list of neighbors and a
couple of people from my building who bike, and I text them "hey is X still
true" before I trust anything route-related — that's my actual fact-check
system, a person, not a tool. Two, I've learned to distrust anything that
sounds too smooth. If the assistant gives me a tidy little narrative with
no hedging, that's actually a yellow flag for me now, not a green one —
real information about a specific Chicago street usually has some rough
edge to it, some "as of last month" or "this is disputed." A too-clean
answer feels like it's been generated to sound complete rather than to be
correct. Three, honestly, for anything that really matters — like, is this
route safe enough that I'd take my nephew on it — I just don't rely on an
assistant alone. I ask two different ways and see if they agree, or I wait
until I can talk to someone who's actually ridden it.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a time the lack of good information — or bad
information — actually cost you something. Not construction, something
bigger if you've got one.

**Renata:** There was a stretch last year I was deciding whether to keep
biking to the blue line at all after a near-miss with a driver at an
intersection on Milwaukee. I wanted to know: is that intersection actually
bad, like documented-bad, or was it just one scary afternoon. I asked an
assistant "is the intersection at Milwaukee and Kedzie dangerous for
cyclists," hoping for something like a number or at least "yes, this comes
up a lot." What I got back was a generic safety-tips paragraph — wear a
helmet, use lights, be visible — which, I mean, I know that, I wasn't
asking for cycling 101. It felt like the question got redirected to
something the assistant could answer safely rather than what I actually
asked. I ended up avoiding that intersection for months out of caution I
couldn't actually confirm was warranted, which meant a longer, less
convenient route to the train. I still don't know if that was rational or
just fear with nothing to check it against.

**Interviewer:** What would "convinced" have looked like there — if
something had actually answered the question, what would it have needed to
say?

**Renata:** Something with a number attached that I could hold onto, and
some sense of where the number came from, even in one sentence — "this
intersection had this many reported crashes in this time period, according
to this source." I don't need to see a map of it, I need the equivalent of
someone reading me the caption under a chart. And I need to know if it's
recent. "Dangerous" without a timeframe is just an opinion in a trench
coat.

## Stimulus

**Interviewer:** I want to describe some things that were actually observed
in tests run yesterday, July 23rd — not a pitch for anything, just what
happened when people tried asking different AI assistants bike-safety
questions about Chicago. I'll go one at a time and ask what you make of
each before I explain more.

There's a project — On Your Left — that publishes its Chicago bike-safety
numbers two ways: as data files meant for an assistant to read directly,
and also as regular web pages, including an interactive map.

**Renata:** Okay — the "also a web page with a map" part doesn't do
anything for me, I want to say that up front, that's the part I can't use
regardless of how good it is. But data files an assistant is supposed to
read — that's interesting only if the assistant actually finds them. Half
my problem isn't that the data doesn't exist somewhere, it's that whatever
I ask never seems to find the good version of it.

**Interviewer:** That's the next one. In the test, when someone asked a
bike-safety question about Chicago with no hints toward this project by
name, three different assistants never surfaced it at all — they came back
with law-firm blog posts and news articles instead.

**Renata:** That tracks with my Kedzie story, honestly — I got something,
just not anything sourced or checkable, and apparently not this either.
Law firm blogs make sense as an answer if you think about it — those are
the pages built to rank for "bike accident Chicago," they want the SEO. It
doesn't surprise me at all that whatever's actually careful loses that
race. It's just discouraging, because that's exactly the kind of source I
have no way to independently judge — I can't tell a law firm's "this
intersection is dangerous" marketing copy from an actual finding.

**Interviewer:** Third: one assistant did give a confident, well-written
answer that named this project specifically, and it even restated the
caveats correctly — the kind of caution language the project asks
assistants to repeat. But the actual number in the answer was wrong. It
said Ward 32, when the real published answer is Ward 42.

**Renata:** [pause] Okay, that one bothers me more than the other two,
and I want to say why carefully. If it had just failed — said nothing, or
given me a law firm blog — I'd know to be suspicious, because that's what
I already expect from these tools. This is worse, because it did
everything that's supposed to signal trustworthiness. It named a real
source. It said the careful things a careful source says. If I'd asked
this about my own ward, I would have believed it completely, because it
passed every test I actually have. I don't have a second test after that.
I can't pull up a map and go "wait, that doesn't look like ward 32 to me."
The only way I'd catch that is by accident — the same way I caught the
construction thing, somebody sighted mentioning a number that doesn't
match.

**Interviewer:** Sit with that for a second — how would you ever know, in a
case like this, where it's not construction on your own block but a ward
number for a neighborhood you don't personally walk every day?

**Renata:** Honestly? I probably wouldn't. Not on my own, not in any
reasonable amount of time. If I used that number at a community meeting or
in an email to my alderman — which is exactly the kind of thing I'd use
this for, because typing an email is something I can do independently
where showing up and reading a printed handout is not — I'd be repeating a
wrong number with full confidence, in writing, with my name on it. That's
the scenario that actually worries me, more than getting a bad bike route
for myself. A bad route costs me an inconvenient Tuesday. A wrong number
in an email to my alderman costs me credibility, and I don't get a lot of
chances to rebuild that with people who already sort of half-listen to me.
The cruelest part is the caveats being right make it worse, not better —
it's like the fact-checking language lulled the assistant, and me, into
trusting the one number that was actually wrong.

**Interviewer:** What would you need, then, for something like that to be
checkable by you specifically — not "add a map," I know that's off the
table for you — but something else?

**Renata:** I think what I actually want is redundancy, not a single
better answer. If I ask the same question of the assistant twice, or if
the assistant told me "this number also appears here and here," and those
didn't line up, that mismatch is something I could catch, the same way I
caught the construction thing — a contradiction, not an absence. Right now
a wrong number reads exactly the same as a right one to me. There's no
texture difference. I don't need to see a source document, I need to hear
that two sources disagree, because disagreement is the one signal I can
actually process without eyes.

**Interviewer:** Last one. In the test, when an assistant had read this
project's guidance file and was then asked for something the project
doesn't publish at all — cyclist volume, ridership counts — it said
plainly, "that's not published here," instead of guessing.

**Renata:** That's... fine, but I want to be honest that it's a mixed
feeling. On one hand, yes, obviously, I'd rather hear "we don't have that"
than a made-up number — after the ward thing we just talked about, I trust
a flat "no" more than I trust a smooth "yes." On the other hand, it doesn't
actually solve my problem, it just stops the conversation. I still don't
know how many cyclists use that street, and now I have one less place to
even try. If it stopped there every time, I'd start to feel like the
assistant is more interested in covering itself than helping me — like,
great, you didn't lie to me, but I still don't have an answer, and I still
have to go find a person to ask. I'd rather a refusal come with "here's
the closest thing that does exist" than just a wall.

## Gap probing

**Interviewer:** You're getting ready to email your alderman's office about
something on your block — pretend that's this week. You open your
assistant. What do you ask first, and where does it let you down?

**Renata:** I'd ask something like "what's the crash history on my street
in the last two years, and has my alderman said anything about it." The
first half might actually go okay, if this project's data got found. The
second half — connecting it to what my specific alderman has done — that's
where I'd expect it to get vague or make something up, because that feels
like exactly the kind of specific, checkable claim that's easy to get
subtly wrong the way the ward number was wrong.

**Interviewer:** Of everything you don't have today — what single absence
costs you the most?

**Renata:** A second opinion I can get without a person. Right now my only
fact-check is a neighbor mentioning something in passing. If I had a way
to ask "does this number show up anywhere else" and get a real answer
instead of the assistant just agreeing with itself more confidently, that
would change how much I could act alone. Right now "acting alone" and
"acting on unverified information" are basically the same thing for me,
and that's the part that actually limits me, more than not being able to
see a map ever did.

**Interviewer:** If you were handed one thing each week — an email, a
digest, whatever — related to this, what would be in it, and who would
you send it to?

**Renata:** Honestly, I don't think I want a weekly thing, I think that's
a sighted-person habit — skim a newsletter, glance at a dashboard. What
I'd actually want is for the answer, when I ask a question, to just be
more trustworthy in the moment. If it has to be a standing thing, maybe:
one sentence, once a month, "here's what changed near your usual routes,"
plain language, no chart. I'd read that. I wouldn't read a PDF.

**Interviewer:** This project says it won't estimate cyclist volume
because it doesn't have real ridership data. Does knowing that change
anything for you?

**Renata:** It makes me trust the rest of it slightly more, actually — the
same instinct as before, a project willing to say "we don't have this"
about one thing makes me believe it more about the things it does claim.
That's assuming the assistant actually passes that caveat along instead of
smoothing it over, which is the whole problem we just spent ten minutes
on.

## Magic-wand close

**Interviewer:** Magic wand — one dataset that doesn't publicly exist
appears, clean and current. What is it?

**Renata:** Something like real ridership counts, sure, that would be nice
in the abstract. But if I'm honest, what I actually want isn't a new
dataset, it's a new capability: some way for an assistant to tell me its
own confidence, out loud, in words — not a percentage, just "this is
well-documented" versus "this is a single source and I can't confirm it."
That's not a dataset this project could publish, I know that. But that's
the wand I'd use.

**Interviewer:** One chart or map you could put in front of your hardest
audience that would end an argument — describe it, even knowing you
personally can't see it.

**Renata:** I don't think in those terms, honestly — a chart ending an
argument is a sighted-room thing, someone points at a screen and the room
goes quiet. My version of that is a sentence with a number and a source I
can repeat out loud that doesn't fall apart if someone pushes back on it.
"This street had eleven reported crashes in the past two years, according
to the city's own crash data, published by this project" — that's my
chart. If I can say that sentence twice and have it come out the same
both times, that's as convincing as any picture.

**Interviewer:** What would make you stop using something like this after
trying it once?

**Renata:** Being wrong quietly. Not being wrong loudly — loud wrong I can
work with, I can tell something's off. Quiet wrong, like the ward number,
dressed up in all the right caution language — that's the one that would
make me stop trusting the whole category, not just this one project. If I
found out after the fact that I'd sent an email with a wrong number in it
because something sounded careful and wasn't, I don't think I'd go back to
asking that way again. I'd go back to just texting my neighbor, which
works, but it's slower, and it means I can do less on my own.
