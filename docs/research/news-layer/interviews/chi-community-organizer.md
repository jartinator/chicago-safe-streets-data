# Interview: chi-community-organizer — news-coverage concept (2026-07-13)

*Persona: Andre Thompson, West Side (Austin) community organizer and block-club coalition coordinator. Interview conducted in character per `docs/research/user-needs/personas/chi-community-organizer.md` and `_shared-rules.md`.*

---

**Q1. Tell me about the last time a news story mattered to your organizing — what was it, how did your people encounter it, what did you do with it?**

Back in the spring — March, I want to say — Block Club ran a piece on a kid who got hit crossing Chicago Avenue near Laramie. Fourteen years old, walking to the bus. Wasn't even a bike story, but it's our corridor, so it landed the same way. I didn't see it first. Miss Odessa from the block club on Latrobe saw it on her phone, screenshotted the headline, and it went around three or four group texts before I even opened Block Club myself. That's how news moves on the West Side — it doesn't come to you cold off a website, it comes to you because somebody's auntie forwarded it with "did y'all see this."

I printed that article — actually printed it, at the library because my printer was out of toner — and brought it to the next task force meeting with CDOT. Didn't need to say much. Laid it on the table next to the crash numbers we already had. That's the difference between a story and a number: the number says "38th percentile for pedestrian injuries," the story says a name and a bus stop. People lean forward for the second one.

**Q2. West Side coverage: when something happens on your streets — a crash, a promised project stalling — does it get written about? How do you find out, and what's missing?**

Depends what "it" is. Somebody dies, sometimes it gets covered — one paragraph, maybe two, and then nothing. Nobody comes back six weeks later to ask what CDOT did about it. That follow-up story basically doesn't exist for us. Meanwhile a stalled project — say the Division Street protected lane that's been "under review" since I don't know when — that almost never makes the paper unless somebody like me calls a reporter directly and practically hands them the story. I've done that. Called a guy at Streetsblog twice, got one callback.

Mostly I find out things happened through the block club phone tree, through the alderman's office newsletter — which is really just a PDF somebody's intern makes — or because somebody posted it in the Austin Coming Together Facebook group. Press is downstream of us, not the other way around. What's missing is anybody assigned to just watch these streets the way somebody's watching Lincoln Park or Logan Square. We're a story when we're a tragedy. We're not a story when we're a pattern.

**Q3. [Stimulus read.] React honestly. What would you use, ignore, distrust?**

[reacts]

Okay, hold on — when you say it matches on "ward, alderman, street name," my first thought was it's reading the whole article and figuring out if it's actually about the ordinance we're fighting. That's not what you said. You said it's just catching mentions. That's a different thing and honestly the more honest thing, so — good, keep it that way once you correct me.

The "Recent coverage for this ward" box next to the task-force meeting list — I'd actually use that. Not for myself, for the folks I bring to meetings who don't follow this stuff day to day. If it's sitting right there next to "here's the next CDOT meeting," that's useful, that's the kind of thing I screenshot and text out.

The "In the news" box just sitting on the ward page by itself — I'd probably scroll past it most days. I don't send my neighbors to a dashboard to read headlines; I bring them one printout. And that little badge distinguishing "real" headlines from "derived" matching — I get why you built it, but nobody in a folding chair at Greater St. John is going to care about that distinction. That's for you, not for us.

What I'd distrust on sight: five years of watching Austin get less coverage than Lincoln Park, I already know what that box is going to look like most weeks. One stale headline. So my first read of an empty or thin box isn't "huh, interesting" — it's "yeah, that tracks."

**Q4. Outlets cover some neighborhoods far more than others. If your ward's page shows one stale headline while a North Side ward shows five fresh ones — what does that say to your members? Does showing coverage gaps help you or hurt you?**

It says what they already believe, which is: nobody's watching. I've had a grandmother tell me straight up that she thinks the news only covers us when something bad happens, and that box would prove her right in about four seconds. So there's a version of this where it just becomes another deficit map — Austin looks empty, looks quiet, looks like nothing's happening here, when really it's that nobody sent a reporter. Silence on the page reads as silence on the ground if you don't say otherwise.

But — and I go back and forth on this — a receipt's a receipt. If I can screenshot "Ward 29: 1 story, 90 days" next to "Ward 43: 5 stories" and put that in front of an editor, that's leverage I didn't have before. I've had those conversations before with no proof, just me on the phone getting told "we cover what's newsworthy." Now I've got something to hold up. So it can cut either way depending on who's holding it and what they say next to it. If nobody explains why the box is empty, it hurts us. If I'm the one explaining it, it helps me.

**Q5. One headline in the list is matched wrong — a street in the neighboring ward. What does that do for you?**

That's it, that's the whole trust gone for that meeting. Somebody in the room — and it's always somebody, we've got a retired CTA guy who catches everything — is going to say "that's not even our ward, that's Cicero Avenue over in 37," and now I'm not explaining the story, I'm explaining the tool. I don't get that time back.

And I'll be honest, it doesn't read to me as neutral. When the computer gets something wrong about Austin, I don't file it under "glitch," I file it under "of course." We've had census tracts miscoded, we've had crash locations pinned to the wrong intersection on maps the city itself put out — it's always us it happens to, or it's always us that notices because nobody double-checked before it went live over here. One wrong match and I stop trusting the other four headlines too, even if they're right.

**Q6. The feature deliberately does NOT link stories to specific upcoming meetings or ordinances. For the task-force meetings you attend: would you want it to try, even if sometimes wrong?**

No. And I want to be clear that's not me being cautious for caution's sake — you just showed me street matching can put a story in the wrong ward. Now imagine that same guessing tries to tell my task force "this article is about your agenda item 4B" and it's wrong. Somebody stands up at CDOT and reads that headline into the record thinking it's about our bikeway extension, and it turns out it's about a totally different ordinance three wards over. That's not a glitch anymore, that's me losing credibility in front of the people I need to believe me.

I already do that matching myself — that's my job, that's what "who's at the table" means, I'm the one who knows this story is actually about the ordinance we're fighting and that one isn't. I don't need a tool guessing at that and getting it wrong on my worst day. Keep the two things apart. Show me the headline, show me the meeting, let me draw the line.

**Q7. Magic wand: one thing about news + this dashboard you'd change or add?**

Don't let an empty box just sit there blank like nothing happened. If Austin's had zero stories in ninety days, say that plainly — "no coverage found for this ward in the last 90 days" — instead of just showing nothing and letting people guess whether that means peace and quiet or means nobody's watching. I've spent twenty years fighting the idea that no data means no problem. Don't let your dashboard make that same mistake by accident. Say the silence out loud, and then I can use it.
