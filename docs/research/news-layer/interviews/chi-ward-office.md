# Interview: chi-ward-office — news-coverage concept (2026-07-13)

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time press coverage — Streetsblog, Block Club, anyone — changed a day in your office. What ran, who saw it first, what did you have to do?

**chi-ward-office:** Six weeks back, a Tuesday. Block Club ran a piece on a dooring near the Armitage Brown Line stop — not fatal, but the cyclist was a teacher at a school in our ward, so it had a name and a face, not just a corridor. I did not see it first. I found out because another chief of staff, a guy I know from a neighboring ward, texted me the link at 8:40 in the morning with "this near you?" before I'd had coffee. The piece didn't even quote my alderwoman — it quoted an advocate saying the corridor's been on the "informal ask" list for two years with no movement, which is true, and which is exactly the sentence that gets repeated back to us at the next CAPS meeting. So I spent the first hour of my day not on the fifty other things queued up, but on: calling Andrea at CDOT to ask if there's actually a timeline nobody told me about, drafting four sentences my alderwoman could say if a reporter called her directly, and deciding whether we proactively put out our own statement or just wait to see if it got legs. It didn't get picked up further, so we sat on it. But that first hour was gone, and it was gone because a colleague happened to see it before I did, not because I had a system.

**Interviewer:** How do you currently track what's being written about your ward and your alder on transportation? Walk me through the actual routine, tools and all. What breaks?

**chi-ward-office:** Honestly, it's a Google Alert, and I've told you before I only half-trust it. I've got one running on my alderwoman's name plus "bike lane," and a second one on two or three of our main streets plus "cyclist" or "crash," because if I just alert on the street name alone I get flooded — one of our streets shares a name with something in three other states, so I get Ohio zoning meetings in my inbox some weeks. Block Club's own site search is decent if I already suspect something ran, but I don't proactively browse it, I don't have time. Streetsblog Chicago I read maybe once a week, skimming headlines, because their coverage tends to be citywide policy stuff more than block-level, and I'm looking for our ward specifically. And then there's the informal network — other chiefs of staff, my CDOT liaison, sometimes even a block club president who reads more than I do and forwards me something with "did you see this." What breaks: the alert misses things constantly, especially if Block Club doesn't put the street name or ward number in the headline, just in paragraph three. And there's no archive — if something ran two months ago and a resident references it, I'm searching my own inbox trying to remember if I ever actually saw it or just heard about it secondhand.

**Interviewer:** [Stimulus read: an "In the news" ward-page section, up to five headlines from the last ~90 days from Streetsblog and Block Club matched by ward number, alderman name, or street name; also appears on the "Take action" page as "Recent coverage for this ward"; no summarizing or claiming a story is about a specific meeting; matching by ward/alder/street name can occasionally be wrong; real headlines badged real, matching badged derived.] React honestly. What would you actually use, ignore, distrust?

**chi-ward-office:** The ward-page version, I'd actually use — that's the thing I just told you I half-do with a Google Alert and a network of favors. If it's genuinely scoped to my ward number, my alderwoman's name, and our streets, and it's just five headlines with a date and a link, that's less work than what I do now, and I'd check it instead of trusting Ohio zoning meetings to filter themselves out of my inbox. I'd probably still keep the Google Alert running for a while out of habit — old habits don't die because a new tool showed up, they die because the new tool burns me or saves me enough times in a row that I stop checking the old one.

The second listing, on the Take Action page next to the committee meetings — I'd ignore that one completely. It's the same five headlines, I've already seen them on the ward page, and I don't browse this site for fun, I go to whatever page answers my question and leave. If I'm on the meetings page I'm there for meeting dates, not news I already checked twenty minutes earlier. That's not a complaint about the feature, that's just — I'm not going to look at the same list twice because you put it in two places.

The "matching can occasionally be wrong" line is the one I circled mentally the second you said it, because that's exactly the sentence that turns a convenience into a liability for someone in my seat. I'll get to why.

**Interviewer:** Your alder's name shows up in that ward-page list under a headline they'd hate. A resident emails you a screenshot: "even the city's bike dashboard says…" (it's not the city's, but that's what the email says). Walk me through what happens and how that affects your view of the dashboard.

**chi-ward-office:** First thing I do is not correct the resident, at least not first — I read what the headline actually says, because the resident's paraphrase and the actual article are usually two different things, and I've been burned assuming they match. Say it's the Armitage dooring piece again, and now instead of my colleague texting me, it's sitting on OYL's ward page and a resident's forwarded me a screenshot of it with "even the city's bike dashboard says you've done nothing on this corridor in two years." I'd write back and correct the "city's" part — it's not the city, it's an independent site, I'd say that plainly — but I already know from the Ward Wise ranking two years ago that the correction barely matters to the resident. They didn't email me because they care who publishes it. They emailed me because it confirmed something they already believed, and now they've got a link.

So what actually happens is the same thing that happens with any bad headline: I read the underlying article carefully, because if it's accurate I can't spin it, I have to answer it — timeline, what we're actually waiting on, who to blame if there's someone to blame. If it's the advocate-quote situation, no direct quote from my alderwoman, I say that too, factually, "she wasn't asked to comment," because sometimes that detail matters to a resident who assumes silence means agreement.

Does it change my view of the dashboard? A little, and not in the dashboard's favor, even though I know that's not entirely fair to you. It's not that OYL did anything wrong — it surfaced a real headline, badged as real, didn't claim anything false. But from where I sit, it's now a second place the same bad headline lives, findable by anyone who types in our ward, forever, or at least for ninety days. Before, a resident had to have already read Block Club or had a friend forward it. Now they can just pull up our ward page. That's not a data-quality problem, that's a distribution problem, and it's still my problem either way.

**Interviewer:** One of the five headlines is matched wrong — it's about a street in the neighboring ward. What does what to your use of the feature?

**chi-ward-office:** That one costs you more than you'd think, because of exactly what I do with information like this — I forward it. If I see a "Recent coverage" list and something in there looks relevant, my instinct is to screenshot it into an email to my alderwoman, the same way I'd flag the dooring undercount card, and now I've handed her a headline about a street that isn't even in our ward, with her name nowhere near the actual story. If she reads it fast, in the car, between two events — which is the only way she reads anything I send her — she could walk into a meeting referencing an article that isn't even about us. That's a worse failure than the alert missing something. A miss just means I'm back to where I was before. A wrong match means I might actively embarrass her with something I handed her myself.

So practically, once I catch one wrong match, I stop trusting the list at face value — I start opening every one of the five links myself before I forward anything, which is maybe two extra minutes, fine, except now I'm doing the exact verification work the feature was supposed to save me. It doesn't make me stop using it, because five links to check is still faster than my Google Alert plus a network of texts. But it moves the feature from "here's your Monday briefing item, forward it" to "here's a list I still have to fact-check before it touches my alderwoman's inbox." That's a real downgrade, even if it's still net useful.

**Interviewer:** The feature deliberately does NOT link stories to specific upcoming meetings or ordinances. As someone who preps an alder for committee: would you want it to try, even if sometimes wrong?

**chi-ward-office:** No — and I feel strongly enough about that to push back on the premise a little. You already told me the ward-street matching is sometimes wrong, and that's a soft failure — worst case, I forward a headline that's not about us, mildly embarrassing, I catch it eventually. Tying a headline to a specific ordinance or a specific Tuesday's committee vote is a hard failure if it's wrong, because that's not "here's some background," that's a claim about what a vote is about, and I've told you before — I need committee vote records, not sponsorship counts, because I don't stake anything on a number I can't defend if someone in the room pushes back. If OYL says "this article relates to the ordinance up for a vote Thursday" and it doesn't, and my alderwoman repeats that connection out loud in Ped and Traffic Safety, or worse, a hostile alder or a reporter catches the mismatch, that's not my problem anymore, that's my alderwoman's problem, on the record, in the room. I do that linkage myself right now, by hand, because I'm the one who knows whether a story about "reckless bike lane rollout" is actually about the ordinance on Thursday's agenda or about something from eight months ago that just got mentioned in passing. That's judgment, not matching. Leave it as headline-plus-link and let me do the connecting — I'd rather have five things I have to think about than one thing that thinks for me and is sometimes wrong in a way I don't catch until it's already out of my mouth.

**Interviewer:** Magic wand: one thing about news + this dashboard you'd change or add?

**chi-ward-office:** Push it to me, don't make me remember to check it. I already told you I've got a weekly ask for OYL — a one-page digest that lands in the same folder as my constituent-call tally for the Monday briefing. Fold this straight into that: if any new ward-tagged headline showed up in the last week, put it at the top of that digest, don't make it a page I have to think to open. Right now even my half-trusted Google Alert at least shows up in my inbox uninvited — this feature, as you've described it, only works if I remember it exists and go looking, and bike safety gets maybe forty minutes of my week. The five-headline list on the ward page is the right shape. What's missing is it finding me instead of me finding it.

If I get a second wish — some kind of small history so a headline that ages out after ninety days doesn't just vanish. Same reason I screenshot the Ward Wise map every quarter: sources change under me, and if a resident references something from four months ago that's dropped off the list, I want to be able to confirm it existed and what it actually said, not just take their word for it.
