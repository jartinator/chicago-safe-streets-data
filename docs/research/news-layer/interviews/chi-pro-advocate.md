# Interview: chi-pro-advocate — news-coverage concept (2026-07-13)

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time you used a news article in your actual work — testimony, a memo, a campaign. Which article, why that one, and what did it do that your data couldn't?

**Reyna:** Streetsblog's piece on the 18th Street teardown, the one from last spring where they got Dowell's office on the record saying the protected intersection was "confusing drivers." I put a link to that article in the cover email when I sent our testimony packet to three other committee members, not in the testimony itself — you don't cite Streetsblog in testimony, that's a rookie move, CDOT engineers will roll their eyes. But in the email I said something like "for background, here's what the alderman told the press," because our crash data and CDOT's design rationale couldn't tell anyone *why* Dowell wanted it gone or that she'd said it in public where it's now on the record. The data told me a protected intersection existed and got removed. The article told me the political story — the quote, the timeline, that it was covered before it was voted on. That's the thing a number can't do: it can't tell you what someone said, on the record, before they had to answer for it in committee.

**Interviewer:** How do you track bike/street coverage across outlets today? The real routine — feeds, alerts, interns, whatever. Where does it fail you?

**Reyna:** Honestly, it's a Google Alert for "Chicago bike lane" plus "Chicago protected lane" that dumps into a folder I check maybe twice a week, and then Marcus forwards me things from Streetsblog Chicago's newsletter when he catches them before I do. We don't have an intern doing media monitoring — we had one two summers ago, Priya, and when her term ended nobody picked it back up, so it went back to "whoever notices first." It fails constantly. I found out about the February ordinance dying at full Council from a colleague texting me a Block Club headline, not from anything I was tracking myself — I was in a different meeting when it happened. And it's ward-blind. If something runs about, say, the 41st Ward that I'm not currently working, I will never see it, because my Google Alert is topic-based, not ward-based, and I don't have the bandwidth to run fifty saved searches. So when a ward office calls me cold and says "did you see the Streetsblog piece about our corridor," half the time the honest answer is no, and I have to go find it live on the call, which looks bad.

**Interviewer:** [Stimulus read: OYL considering an "In the news" section — up to five recent headlines per ward from outlets like Streetsblog and Block Club, matched by ward number/alderman name/street name, appearing on the ward page and near the meetings list on Take Action, headline/outlet/date only, no summarizing, matching can occasionally be wrong, badged real vs. derived.] React honestly. What would you use, ignore, distrust?

**Reyna:** Okay — my first reaction is actually relief, because this is the exact gap I just described: I don't have ward-based monitoring and you're describing ward-based monitoring. If I pull up the 15th Ward page before a ward night and there's a little "recent coverage" strip with a Streetsblog headline about Archer Avenue sitting right next to the meeting list, that's genuinely useful — that's the thing I currently find out about from a text message or not at all. I'd use it as a pre-meeting scan, absolutely, every time. What I'd ignore: if it's just five headlines with no way to tell why each one matched — like, does "matches ward 15" mean the alderman was quoted, or just that a street in the ward got mentioned in passing in a citywide roundup? I'd want to hover or click through before I trust it means anything. And what I'd distrust immediately, on sight, is if a headline shows up that's clearly about the wrong thing — like if "Archer Avenue" pulled in a story about a restaurant opening that happens to be on Archer, that tells me the matching is dumb string-matching and I have to eyeball every single item before I'd ever forward one.

**Interviewer:** You know Streetsblog's editorial position on bike infrastructure. An evidence dashboard that lists Streetsblog headlines next to official crash data — does that mix worry you, help you, or neither? Would opponents use it against the dashboard?

**Reyna:** It doesn't worry me for my own use — I already know Streetsblog leans pro-bike-lane, everyone in this world knows that, it's like knowing the Sun-Times editorial board leans a certain way. I read past it fine. But you asked would opponents use it, and yes, obviously, that's not hypothetical for me — I've sat across from a 41st Ward-type alderman's chief of staff who called Streetsblog "the bike lobby's newsletter" to my face, and if OYL's ward page has a Streetsblog headline sitting right next to a KSI number with no visible difference in styling, that staffer is going to say the whole site is "just Streetsblog with extra steps," and use that to wave off the crash data too, which is data I need them to take seriously. The badge system helps some — if "real" just means "yes, this outlet published this headline" and doesn't imply OYL endorses the framing, I can say that out loud in the room. But you need Block Club in there too, genuinely, not as a token — Block Club reads as more neutral in most rooms I'm in, and if the ward strip is four Streetsblog headlines and one Block Club, that ratio itself becomes the thing a skeptical staffer points at.

**Interviewer:** One headline in a ward list is matched wrong (neighboring ward's street). What does that do for you, given how you'd actually use this?

**Reyna:** It does the exact thing we talked about with the ward table in the first study — one bad number and I stop trusting the batch. Same principle, honestly worse, because with a wrong crash number I can at least defend myself with "here's the methodology, here's the date cutoff." With a wrong headline match, there's no methodology to recite — it's either right or it's visibly, embarrassingly wrong, and everyone in the room can see it's wrong just by reading the headline, no data literacy required. If I'm doing a ward night in the 15th and one of the five headlines is actually about a street two wards over, and someone in the audience knows that street isn't in our ward — and in my rooms, someone always knows the ward boundaries better than I'd like — I don't get to say "well, four out of five were right." I get "if they can't even get the ward right, why would I trust their crash numbers." It contaminates the whole page, not just the news strip. So practically: I'd screen every headline before I ever put the page up on a shared screen, which eats back most of the time this was supposed to save me.

**Interviewer:** The feature deliberately does NOT link stories to specific upcoming meetings or ordinances. You prep public comment for committee hearings: would you want it to try, even if sometimes wrong?

**Reyna:** No — and I want to be clear that's not me being cautious for caution's sake, it's the February ordinance lesson applied to a new place. If OYL had told me back then "this Streetsblog article is about the citizen-reporting ordinance," and it was actually about a different, similar-sounding blockage bill from the year before, I could've cited the wrong article in testimony and had a committee member's staffer catch it and use it to say I hadn't done my homework. A wrong ward-street match embarrasses me in a room. A wrong article-to-ordinance match could get into a written record. I'll do that linking work myself — I already read the agenda, I already know which article is about which bill, that's actually a part of my job I'm good at and don't want automated badly. Keep it dumb — headline, outlet, date, "here's what mentions this ward" — and let me do the interpretive work. The one place I'd make an exception: if it's genuinely unambiguous, like the article's headline literally contains the ordinance's own bill number, sure, fine. But "probably about this meeting" is worse than nothing.

**Interviewer:** Magic wand: one thing about news + this dashboard you'd change or add?

**Reyna:** A ward-scoped version of what I already do badly with my Google Alert — but let me subscribe to it, don't make me remember to check the page. If I could get a once-a-week email, one per ward I'm actively working that quarter, just the headlines that matched that ward in the last seven days, I would use that constantly, because it solves the actual failure mode I described earlier: finding out about coverage from a colleague's text instead of catching it myself. I don't need it on the site only — the site is something I have to remember to open, and I don't, not on a regular cadence, same as I don't check my own Google Alert folder as often as I should. Push it to me and I'll read every one.

## Summary

- **Overall verdict: use, with a screening ritual.** She adopts the ward-page news strip readily — it fills a real, named gap (no ward-based monitoring today, only a topic Google Alert plus colleague texts) — but treats it the way she treats the ward table from the earlier study: every headline gets eyeballed before it goes on a shared screen, because a wrong match is instantly, publicly obvious with zero data literacy required to catch it, unlike a crash-count discrepancy she can at least explain with methodology.
- **Strongest quote:** "With a wrong crash number I can at least defend myself with 'here's the methodology, here's the date cutoff.' With a wrong headline match, there's no methodology to recite — it's either right or it's visibly, embarrassingly wrong."
- **Genuinely didn't interest her / pushed back hard on:** linking articles to specific meetings or ordinances, even the stimulus's own "does not do this" design choice she independently re-derives and endorses — she wants that interpretive step to stay hers, calling it "a part of my job I'm good at and don't want automated badly." She also flagged, unprompted, that an all-Streetsblog or Streetsblog-heavy ward strip becomes ammunition for opponents who already call it "the bike lobby's newsletter," and wants Block Club represented for optics, not just coverage.
