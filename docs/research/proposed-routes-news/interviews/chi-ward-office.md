# Interview: chi-ward-office — proposed-projects concept (2026-07-13)

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time a constituent asked your office about a project that was rumored or announced but not actually built. What did they believe, what was actually true, and what did it cost you to find out?

**chi-ward-office:** Two months ago, guy emails the general inbox — subject line "WHERE IS MY BIKE LANE," all caps, so I know it's going to be a whole thing. He's talking about a stretch on our end of the lakefront-adjacent corridor that got a mention in a CDOT press thing back in 2023, something about a "planned protected connection." He'd seen it on a neighborhood Facebook group, somebody had screenshotted an old rendering, and by the time it got to him it had turned into "the city already approved this, why hasn't it happened." What was actually true: it was never funded, it was a concept in a plan document, one of maybe forty corridors CDOT floated in the Cycling Strategy update. Nothing was ever "planned" in the sense he meant. Finding that out cost me an hour and a half — I had to call Andrea, our CDOT liaison, because I genuinely couldn't tell from the press release language whether it had graduated from wish-list to project. She had to go check with someone on her end too. Then I had to write him back in a way that didn't sound like I was blowing him off, because "it was aspirational" reads to a constituent like "we lied to you." That's the actual cost — not the hour and a half, the reply. I probably rewrote that email four times.

**Interviewer:** How does your office track the actual status of CDOT projects in your ward today? Walk me through the real routine and where it breaks.

**chi-ward-office:** The scar sheet, which you've probably heard about if you've talked to me before — Google Sheet, project name, what was promised, date, source of the promise, status, date of last movement, who to call. That's the backbone. It gets updated when I'm forced to, which is mostly when a resident brings something up and I realize I don't actually know the current state. For live status specifically, the pre-brief with Andrea is the real mechanism — I call her, or email if it's not urgent, and ask "where's this at." That works fine when I remember to ask and she has time to check. It breaks in two places. One, CDOT's internal status and what actually gets communicated to me aren't the same clock — I've had projects "on track" in an email from Andrea and then find out three weeks later, from a resident who saw contractors on the block, that scope changed. Two, once something's built or half-built, nobody proactively tells me it's been modified. I find out from a 311 complaint, a phone call, or a reporter. There's no system pushing status changes to me — I'm always pulling, and I only pull when something reminds me to.

**Interviewer:** Let me describe something. On Your Left! may add a "Proposed & in progress" section — a short, hand-maintained list of Chicago's active bike projects, things like the 606 extension, Archer Avenue, Grand Avenue, the DuSable Lake Shore Drive redesign, the Englewood Nature Trail, Weber Spur. Each one shows a volunteer-written status — "in design," "funded in part," "installed, being modified" — with a last-reviewed date, one sentence on what and where, official links, and up to three recent news headlines with the outlet named. Projects with a ward assignment also show a "Proposed here" line on that ward's report. No map lines — they're saying the city doesn't publish a usable planned-routes map, so nothing gets drawn. Empty coverage says "no recent coverage found." React honestly.

**chi-ward-office:** My first reaction is "who's the volunteer." I don't mean that as a throwaway — I mean it literally, because "volunteer-written status" is doing the same job my scar sheet does, except it's somebody I've never met writing about my ward. If it's about Archer or Grand, fine, those are citywide stories, everybody's writing about them, I'll take another set of eyes. But if it ever touches a smaller project in my ward that isn't in the news cycle, I'm going to be skeptical of a status I can't trace to a person or a source I recognize. Half of me wants to use it — most of that roster isn't in my ward anyway, so for me it's basically a Streetsblog digest with structure, which I'd genuinely read, the same way I read the ATA letter. The other half distrusts it on reflex, because "hand-maintained" and "last-reviewed date" together tells me this is one person's best effort on a rolling basis, and best-effort trackers drift — I know because mine does too, and I'm the one being paid to keep it current.

**Interviewer:** A resident emails your alder quoting the site: "even the bike dashboard says the Archer project is 'installed, being modified.'" Your office knows CDOT quietly changed course last week, and the site's status is three weeks stale. Walk me through what happens.

**chi-ward-office:** First thing that happens is I feel a specific kind of annoyed, because now I'm not just answering the resident, I'm correcting a source they trust more than they trust me, and that's a worse position to argue from. I pull up the site myself to see exactly what it says and when it was last touched. If the last-reviewed date is right there, I use it — my reply becomes "that's accurate as of three weeks ago, here's what changed since." That's a fine email to write, actually, better than starting from scratch, because I'm not disputing the resident, I'm updating a shared reference point. But here's the part that costs me: I now also have to decide whether to email whoever runs the site and tell them it's stale, because if I don't, the next resident hits the same wall, and eventually residents start treating "the site says X" as more current than anything my office tells them, which undermines me specifically. That's a new task that didn't used to exist. And if this isn't a one-off — if it happens twice — I stop treating the site as a live source and start treating it like the Ward Wise menu map, something I check but never cite without calling Andrea first anyway.

**Interviewer:** The status is written by volunteers with a last-reviewed date, and the official city link is sitting right there. Does the date plus the link fix question four for you, or not really?

**chi-ward-office:** It fixes the "am I being unfair to the site" problem, not the "resident is upset" problem. The date tells me I'm not dealing with negligence, I'm dealing with lag, and that's a real distinction — I'll extend more patience to a three-week-old volunteer post than to something that's just wrong. And the official link matters because if it goes straight to a CDOT page, I can point the resident there myself and get out of the business of being the arbiter between two data sources. But "fix" is generous. The resident who emailed my alder doesn't care about last-reviewed dates — they saw a status that matched what they wanted to believe, and now I'm the one delivering the less exciting update. The date and the link protect the site's credibility with me. They don't protect my afternoon.

**Interviewer:** A "Proposed here" line on your ward's report — useful for your prep, or another thing to fact-check?

**chi-ward-office:** Depends entirely on whether it's a project I already know about. If it's Archer or something else that's already all over Streetsblog, "Proposed here" is just a index card pointing at a story I already have — mildly convenient, saves me a search, not load-bearing. If it names something smaller I haven't heard of, now it's a fire drill, because I have to find out fast whether it's real before a resident asks me about it and I look like the last to know. So it's not purely useful and it's not purely a burden — it's a notification, and notifications are only as good as how fast I can verify them. If verifying means another call to Andrea, it's just added a step to my week, not removed one. If it comes with a link straight to the CDOT source so I can check in thirty seconds instead of placing a call, that's the difference between "useful" and "another thing to fact-check."

**Interviewer:** Magic wand — one thing about project-status tracking you'd change?

**chi-ward-office:** I want CDOT to tell me when status changes, not the other way around. Not a volunteer noticing, not a resident catching contractors on a block and emailing me, not me remembering to call Andrea every few weeks — CDOT pushing a notice the day scope changes on anything active in my ward. Everything else — the scar sheet, the pre-brief, even a nicely built external tracker like what you're describing — is somebody downstream reacting to a decision that already got made without them. If the actual source fixed that gap, I wouldn't need a volunteer roster to catch what CDOT itself should have told me first.

## Summary

Verdict: cautiously would-use, not would-trust-blind. Marcus reads the concept as "a Streetsblog digest with structure" for the ~5 of 6 example projects outside his ward — genuinely useful there, low stakes if wrong. For anything touching his own ward, his default posture flips to skepticism the instant a status can't be traced to a source he recognizes, because a wrong status becomes his problem to fix, not the site's. He does not treat the last-reviewed date and official link as solving the real cost of staleness — they exonerate the site, not his afternoon.

Strongest quote: "The date and the link protect the site's credibility with me. They don't protect my afternoon."

What genuinely didn't interest him: the roster itself, for projects outside his ward — he'd skim it the way he skims the ATA letter's first line, not something he'd bring to his alder or a community meeting.

Misreading worth flagging: none forced — he correctly inferred "volunteer-written" implies drift risk and correctly distinguished the site's culpability (low, given the date) from the resident-management cost to his office (unchanged), which is a more sophisticated read than the stimulus explicitly argued for.
