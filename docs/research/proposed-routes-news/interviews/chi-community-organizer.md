# Interview: chi-community-organizer — proposed-projects concept (2026-07-13)

**Participant:** Andre Thompson (composite, fictional) — Austin block-club coalition coordinator, CDOT task force member.

---

**Q1. The West Side has a long history with promised projects. Tell me about one your neighbors still bring up — what was promised, what happened, and what people say about it now.**

You want the one that still comes up at every other block-club meeting? The 606 extension. Not "the Bloomingdale Trail" — nobody out here calls it that, that's a Bucktown name for a Bucktown thing. We call it what it was pitched as: the trail coming *our* way.

Back around 2014, 2015, when the Bloomingdale opened, there were renderings — I saw them myself, somebody brought printouts to a CAPS meeting — showing the corridor continuing west along the old rail embankment, past Cicero, toward Austin Boulevard. CDOT staff stood up at a community meeting and talked about "future phases." People got excited. My neighbor Ronnie still has a folder — an actual paper folder — with a flyer from a 2015 open house that says "Phase 2 planning underway."

Then nothing. No funding line, no follow-up meeting, no revised map. It just stopped showing up on anybody's agenda. You ask CDOT about it now and you get "it's a long-term vision," which is department-speak for "don't hold your breath." Meanwhile Bucktown and Logan Square got a finished elevated trail with landscaping and public art, and Austin got a rail embankment that's still fenced off, still collecting garbage.

What people say now — and I mean verbatim, I've heard this exact line from three different people — is "they always got money to extend it toward downtown, never toward us." That's the folk memory. It's not wrong.

---

**Q2. When the city announces something for your area, how do your members find out whether it's actually funded versus just a press release? Walk me through a real chase.**

Real one, not hypothetical: the Austin Boulevard road diet, maybe two years back. Alderman's newsletter says "CDOT has committed to redesigning Austin Boulevard for safety." People forward that around, folks start asking me when the speed humps are coming.

So I do what I always do — I don't trust the newsletter, I go looking for the receipts. First stop is the city's Capital Improvement Program book, the CIP — it's a PDF, it's ugly, but it's the closest thing to a ledger the city keeps public. Austin Boulevard's not in there that year, or it's in there as "planning" money only, which is a different animal than construction money. That's chase number one — a phone call to a task force contact who used to work at CDOT, who tells me "planning money" means somebody drew a line on a map, it doesn't mean cones go in the ground.

Chase number two: I call the alderman's office directly, not the newsletter list — the actual staffer who handles infrastructure. She tells me it's tied to a federal Invest in Cook or RAISE grant application, and the timeline is "if we get the grant." That's the real status: *maybe*, pending an application most of my neighbors have never heard of and can't watch.

That's usually how it goes — press release says "committed," reality is somewhere between "designed" and "hoping." I've learned to ask three questions before I repeat anything at a block club: what phase, whose money, and what's the date. If nobody can answer those three, I tell people it's a maybe, not a yes.

---

**Q3. [Stimulus read.] React honestly — use, ignore, distrust?**

*(pause)*

Okay, mixed, and let me tell you why in order.

First reaction, seeing "the 606 extension" as an actual entry — my stomach did something. Because that's Ronnie's folder, right there, on a website. Somebody's tracking it. That's not nothing. If it says "in design" with a real date next to it, I want to know: design since when? Is this the same "future phase" language from 2015 dressed up new, or is there an actual project number behind it? A status label without knowing who wrote it and when they last checked reads to me exactly like the alderman's newsletter — hopeful, secondhand, not verified.

Archer Avenue, Grand Avenue — not my corridors, I don't have an opinion, but I recognize the shape: those are the fights that get covered because reporters live near them or bike them. Fine.

DuSable Lake Shore Drive — I'll be honest, that one doesn't interest me at all. That's a lakefront mega-project with its own task force, its own consultants, its own coverage in every outlet in the city. It doesn't need me and I don't need it in this list. If I saw that eating space at the top while Austin's project sits below the fold, I'd feel exactly what I always feel — that the glamour project gets the real estate.

Englewood and Weber Spur sitting next to it — I'll get into that next question — but seeing South and Northwest Side projects that don't get press *next to* one that gets endless press, on the same list, with the same format — that's actually the part I respect. That's not how the news treats it. The news treats Lake Shore Drive like it's the only bike story in Chicago.

Overall: I'd use it as a start, not a source. I use it the way I'd use a tip from a neighbor — worth fifteen minutes to go verify, not worth repeating cold.

---

**Q4. Notice the candidate list includes the Englewood Nature Trail and Weber Spur — South and Northwest Side projects with thin press coverage — alongside heavily-covered North Side fights. When an Englewood entry says "funded in part" with "no recent coverage found" underneath, what does that combination say to your members?**

That combination is the whole ballgame, honestly. Let me tell you what two different people in my coalition would hear, because they wouldn't hear the same thing.

One reading — the cynical one, and it's earned — is "funded in part" plus silence means it's already dying and nobody with a microphone noticed. That's the pattern we know: money gets "identified," a groundbreaking photo gets taken, and then it goes quiet because there's no reporter on that beat checking back in eighteen months. Silence isn't peace, it's an unwatched pot. I've watched projects go exactly that route on the West Side — "funded in part," ribbon-cutting, and then radio silence for three years while the "remaining" part never shows.

The other reading, and this is the one I actually push people toward, is: "no recent coverage found" doesn't mean no story exists — it means nobody funded a reporter to go find it. That's the 311 problem all over again, just with news instead of complaints. Englewood not getting metro-section coverage doesn't mean Englewood residents aren't organizing or that CDOT isn't moving — it means the Tribune doesn't send anybody south of Roosevelt unless there's blood in the headline. A sparse-coverage flag next to a South Side project reads to me as "this is where you, the reader, have to go do the legwork the newspaper didn't." If the tool is honest about that being a gap and not proof of nothing happening, that's actually more honest than most maps I've seen — most of them just leave the blank space blank and let you assume it's empty for a reason.

So: it either confirms the worst about how these things die quiet, or it's a pointer telling me where to go make some noise. Probably both, depending on the week.

---

**Q5. The status is volunteer-written with a last-reviewed date and a citation. For a task-force meeting, is that usable evidence, or does "volunteer-written" kill it in the room?**

It doesn't kill it, but it doesn't stand on its own either. Here's how that room actually works — I've sat in it. If I stand up and say "a website says Weber Spur is funded in part," somebody from CDOT's side, or a skeptical alderman staffer, is going to ask me who wrote that and I have to have an answer better than "some volunteer." That sentence, by itself, gets me nowhere. I've had that exact exchange with a spreadsheet a University of Illinois grad student put together — good spreadsheet, real numbers, and it still got waved off because nobody in the room could vouch for the source.

What saves it is the citation underneath. If "funded in part" links straight to an actual CDOT capital document, an IDOT letting notice, a grant award letter — something with a seal on it — then the volunteer did the work I would've had to do myself, and I'll use it, credited as "here's where I found this, here's the primary document." The last-reviewed date matters too, maybe more than people think — I've been burned holding up a printout that was two years stale and getting corrected in the room. A date tells me whether I need to double check it fresh before I say it out loud.

So: volunteer-written plus a real citation is a lead I still verify before I repeat it at the mic. Volunteer-written with a vague or missing link is just a rumor with better formatting. I've got a folder — actual folder, not digital — of things people told me were "basically approved" that turned out to be a slide in somebody's PowerPoint. I'm not adding another one.

---

**Q6. Where should this live — the ward report, its own page, somewhere else — for it to be useful to you? Or is it not for you?**

I'm not going to tell you how to build your website, that's not my job and you didn't ask me to design it. What I'll tell you is how I'd actually go looking for it, because that's the honest part.

I don't browse. Nobody in my coalition browses a civic website for fun. Somebody hears a rumor — "they're doing something on Austin Boulevard" — and they call me, and I need to go confirm or kill that rumor in under five minutes, usually standing in a church basement with my phone at 40% battery. So wherever this lives, if I have to hunt for it separately from wherever I already go to check crash numbers or whatever else is on your site for our ward, I'm not going to remember it exists the third time I need it. If it's attached to the same place I already check for Austin — same click, same page, whatever you call it — I'll use it. If it's a standalone page I have to remember is a separate thing from the ward stuff, it joins the pile of bookmarks nobody opens twice.

---

**Q7. Magic wand: one thing about tracking promised projects you'd change?**

Real dates with teeth, not status words. "In design," "funded in part" — those are soft, and soft is exactly how these things go to die quietly, like we said. I want somebody, anybody, to be able to look at the 606 extension a year from now and say "you told us construction would start by such-and-such date, it's past that date, where's the money." Right now there's no accountability moment — a project just drifts from "in design" to "future vision" and nobody ever has to answer for the drift, because there was never a real promise on the record to hold them to, just a rendering at an open house eleven years ago.

I don't need a fancier map. I need a receipt that outlives the news cycle.

## Summary

**Verdict:** Cautious, conditional use — Andre treats the feature as a lead worth fifteen minutes of verification, never a source to repeat cold. The citation and last-reviewed date are what make it usable at all; without a real primary-source link behind the status word, it's "a rumor with better formatting."

**Strongest quote:** "A date tells me whether I need to double check it fresh before I say it out loud... Volunteer-written with a vague or missing link is just a rumor with better formatting."

**What didn't interest him:** The DuSable Lake Shore Drive redesign — explicitly not his corridor, already over-covered, and its prominence on the list reads to him as the usual pattern of glamour projects crowding out West/South Side ones.

**Misreading/notable interpretation:** He reads "no recent coverage found" on the Englewood entry two ways at once — either confirmation that underfunded projects die quietly unwatched, or (his preferred framing, drawing an explicit parallel to 311 undercount) as an honest admission of a *reporting* gap rather than an *activity* gap, i.e., a pointer to where residents need to do the legwork press won't. He also personalizes the 606 extension as "the Austin promise," projecting his own neighborhood's 2015 open-house history onto whatever "in design" status the tool assigns it — his trust in that one entry will hinge entirely on whether its citation predates or updates that old promise.
