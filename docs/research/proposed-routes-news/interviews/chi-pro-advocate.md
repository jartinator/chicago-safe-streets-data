# Interview: chi-pro-advocate — proposed-projects concept (2026-07-13)

*Participant: Reyna Delgado (composite, fictional), advocacy manager, Chicago
regional active-transportation nonprofit.*

---

**Q1. Tell me about the last time your org had to answer "what's the actual
status of project X?" — for a member, a funder, a reporter. Where did you
look, how long did it take, how confident were you?**

Three weeks ago. A reporter from Block Club — not naming her, but you'd know
the byline — texted me at like 4pm asking if the 606 extension was "still
happening" for a piece she was filing that night. I did not have a clean
answer sitting anywhere. First stop was our internal tracker, which is a
Google Sheet called "Corridor Watch" that Priya on our policy team updates
whenever she remembers to, and it said "design phase, per CDOT briefing
2/2026" — five months old at that point. So then I called my CDOT contact,
who didn't pick up, texted him, waited forty minutes, and while I waited I
went digging through the Active Trans email archive for the last time
someone from Trust for Public Land mentioned it, because they've been
adjacent to that project. Reporter's deadline came before my CDOT guy called
back, so I gave her the Sheet answer with a "as of earlier this year" caveat
and told her to double check with CDOT press directly. She used it. I was
maybe 70% confident in the moment and fully aware I might be one CDOT
internal memo behind. That's a normal Tuesday for us, honestly — that's not
even a bad case.

**Q2. Chicago has no public, structured planned-bikeways tracker (CDOT's is a
spreadsheet). What does that absence cost your work concretely? Any
episode.**

It costs me an afternoon every time someone asks a question that should take
five minutes. Menu money season, last October — a staffer for one of the
Southwest Side alders calls asking whether the segment of Archer they were
about to allocate ward money to would double up with a CDOT-funded piece
already planned two blocks over, because nobody wants to look like they
wasted menu money paying for something the city was already doing. I did not
know. Nobody I called knew off the top of their head. I ended up requesting
the CDOT capital projects list — which is, again, a spreadsheet, columns
like "PID" and "phase" that only make sense if you already know CDOT's
internal shorthand — cross-referencing it by hand against the ward
boundaries, and getting back to the staffer two days later, past the point
where it was useful for their planning conversation. If there were one place
where "here's what's proposed and where it stands" lived and updated, that's
not a two-day turnaround, that's a link I paste into a text message. Instead
I am the tracker. Me and Priya's Sheet are the tracker.

**Q3. [Stimulus read.] React honestly — use, ignore, distrust?**

Okay. Genuine mixed reaction, let me go through it out loud.

The citations-backing-status piece — that I'd actually use. If it says "in
design" and links me the actual CDOT board presentation or the alder's
newsletter where that came from, that's faster than my own process, and I'd
probably paste that link into a ward letter footnote before I'd paste my own
Corridor Watch row, because at least a stranger can click through and verify
it instead of just trusting Priya's memory of a phone call.

But — "hand-maintained by volunteers" is doing a lot of work in that
sentence and it's the first thing I distrust. Hand-maintained by whom? I've
watched well-meaning tech projects show up, demo well, and then the person
who was updating it gets a new job and it sits there telling people
something's "in design" that got value-engineered out eighteen months ago.
"Last-reviewed date" helps, I'll give you that — at least I can see it's
stale instead of trusting it blind — but a stale date doesn't un-mislead
someone who reads the headline and not the fine print.

No map lines: I actually don't mind that, and that surprises me to say. I'd
rather have nothing than a line that's wrong, and I've had to correct
people's mental image of a "planned" bike lane before because some render
somebody saw three years ago never got built as drawn. Being honest about
"nothing machine-readable exists to draw" — fine. That's more integrity than
CDOT's own PDFs usually have.

The news headlines thing I'm lukewarm on. I already have Google Alerts set
for half these corridor names. It might catch the one Sun-Times piece I
missed. It's not nothing, it's just not the reason I'd open this page.

**Q4. The status vocabulary is small and curated ('in design', 'funded in
part', 'blocked'…). You know these projects. Where will that vocabulary
break first, and does a citation next to the status fix it?**

"Funded in part" is going to break constantly, because "funded" in this
city is never one number. Archer is a good example — you could have federal
CMAQ money for one segment, a chunk of ward menu money the alder announced
at a community meeting that hasn't actually hit the books yet, and a City
capital line that's still "proposed" in the budget the Council hasn't voted
on. Is that "funded in part"? Sure, technically, but it's doing a lot of
flattening. I've sat in rooms where "funded" meant three different things to
three different people in the same sentence, and it mattered — funders ask
us "is it funded" and we've learned to never answer that word without
unpacking it.

"Blocked" is the other one. Blocked how? Legally blocked, waiting on IDOT
sign-off? Politically blocked, meaning an alder is sitting on it — which,
again, I watched Ald. Dowell have installed protection torn back out on 18th
Street in real time, that's a kind of "blocked" no spreadsheet cell
captures. Or just construction-sequencing blocked, waiting for a water main
project to finish first. Those are three completely different stories for a
ward letter and "blocked" collapses them into one word.

Does a citation fix it? Partially. If the citation is a specific document —
say, a CDOT board memo that spells out "pending IDOT permit" — that
resolves the ambiguity for that one status, good. But if the citation is
just a news article that also said "blocked" without explaining why, you've
cited the vagueness, not resolved it. I'd trust the status more if I could
tell, from the citation alone, which kind of "blocked" I'm looking at
without having to click through and read the whole article every time.

**Q5. The roster is editorial — six projects picked by the maintainers. What
criteria would make you trust the picks? What omission would make you
suspicious?**

Look at the six you named to me again: the 606 extension, Archer, Grand
Avenue phase 2, DuSable Lake Shore Drive, Englewood Nature Trail, Weber
Spur. That's actually a decent spread — Englewood's a South Side pick, which
tells me somebody thought about it, because the lazy version of this list is
five North Side trail projects and one gesture toward equity. So — good,
some trust extended already.

What would make me suspicious: if next quarter's update quietly drops
Englewood or Weber Spur because they went quiet for a year while
Archer and the 606 get monthly citations because they're the ones with
active press cycles. That's the thing that happens to South and West Side
projects constantly — they don't get abandoned, they just get boring to
cover, and then they fall out of every "here's what's happening" roundup
because nobody's writing about them, and then funders start asking us why
we're not talking about them either, and the honest answer is "because
nobody wrote about it, not because it stalled." If this list drifts toward
whichever six projects have the most news coverage rather than whichever six
matter, that's the same bias I already fight, just imported into a new
tool. I'd want to know the picks track something more like "geographic
spread + a mix of phases" and not just "which ones journalists keep writing
about."

**Q6. You already track these projects better than a volunteer site will.
What, if anything, does this feature give YOU — or is it purely for
residents?**

Honestly, mostly for residents, and I don't say that as an insult — that's
fine, not everything needs to be for me. But there is one use for me
specifically: it's a second, independent-looking source I can point a
skeptical staffer to. When I tell an alder's office "the 606 extension is in
design," that's me, an advocate, telling them what I want to be true — they
half-discount it on reflex. If I can say "and here's a page that isn't us
saying the same thing, with a citation," that's a credibility boost that
costs me nothing to use. I did something like this once with a Sun-Times
clip during a hearing prep — same logic, an outside voice saying the thing I
already knew.

What it doesn't give me is speed on anything I actually need fast, because
my Corridor Watch sheet plus a phone call is still going to beat a
quarterly-reviewed volunteer page on any of the ward-specific menu-money
questions that are my actual daily grind. This is citywide-project status.
My job is ward-by-ward. Those overlap on six corridors and not on the other
forty things I'm tracking in any given month.

**Q7. Magic wand: one thing about the proposed-projects idea you'd change?**

I'd want the last-reviewed date to come with some signal of *why* nothing
changed — was it reviewed and confirmed still true, or reviewed and nobody
could find anything new so it just... sat? Those are different kinds of
"last reviewed," and right now they'd look identical to a reader. A project
that's been "in design" for three years because it's genuinely moving slow
through IDOT and a project that's been "in design" for three years because
whoever curates this page stopped checking on it — I want to be able to
tell those apart before I hand it to a reporter.

If I'm honest, the DuSable Lake Shore Drive redesign doesn't do much for me
either way — it's IDOT's show, it's massive, it already gets wall-to-wall
Tribune and Sun-Times coverage without anyone's help, and it's not the kind
of thing a ward letter or a menu-money hearing ever touches. I'll probably
just skip past that row every time.

## Summary

**Verdict:** Cautiously usable, not decision-critical. Reyna would use the
citation-backed status as a credibility prop with skeptical staffers and
tolerates the no-map-lines honesty, but treats the whole page as secondary
to her own Corridor Watch sheet and phone calls, which are faster for the
ward-specific questions that actually drive her work.

**Strongest quote:** "If this list drifts toward whichever six projects have
the most news coverage rather than whichever six matter, that's the same
bias I already fight, just imported into a new tool."

**What didn't interest her:** The DuSable Lake Shore Drive redesign row
specifically (IDOT-scale, already over-covered by mainstream press, no
ward/menu-money relevance) and the news-headline aggregation feature in
general (she already runs Google Alerts on most of these corridor names).

**Misreading / notable interpretation:** She read "funded in part" and
"blocked" as far more legally/politically loaded terms than the stimulus
likely intends — importing her own world's distinctions (federal CMAQ vs.
ward menu money vs. unpassed capital budget line; legal block vs. political
aldermanic block vs. construction-sequencing block) onto a vocabulary
that's probably meant more loosely. She's explicit that a citation only
partly resolves this, and only if the cited document itself disambiguates
the reason — a news article repeating the vague word doesn't help her.
