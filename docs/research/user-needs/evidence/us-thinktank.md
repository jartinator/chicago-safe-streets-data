## Who the real actors are, and how they operate

**PeopleForBikes** runs the annual **City Ratings** program built on its **Bicycle Network Analysis (BNA)**, a street-level connectivity tool. The BNA scores cities 0-100 (grouped by population size: small/medium/large) using a **Level of Traffic Stress (LTS)** framework originally developed by Maaza Mekuria, Peter Furth, and Hilary Nixon at the Mineta Transportation Institute, aligned with NACTO design standards. It classifies every street/intersection as low- or high-stress based on lane counts/widths, speed limits, signals, and parking presence, then measures how well the "low-stress" network connects people to jobs, schools, parks, grocery stores, and transit, using OpenStreetMap as its base layer. A score of 50 is treated as the connectivity "tipping point" past which a city's low-stress network starts generating real bikeability momentum. The 2026 methodology update tightened LTS criteria, e.g. requiring lower speed limits for buffered/conventional lanes to still count as low-stress (PeopleForBikes City Ratings Methodology; 2026 City Ratings Methodology Updates).

**Vision Zero Network** (the national coalition, distinct from individual city Vision Zero offices) champions the **High Injury Network (HIN)** methodology, pioneered by San Francisco's Department of Public Health, Program on Health, Equity and Sustainability, in 2015 — the first US effort to combine hospital injury data with police crash reports, specifically because roughly 33% of severely injured cyclists, 28% of pedestrians, and 39% of injured vehicle occupants never appear in police records. HINs map the corridors where severe/fatal injuries cluster and are now standard practice across Vision Zero cities, used to prioritize scarce capital dollars (Vision Zero Network, "HIN for the WIN"; SF Vision Zero 2022 HIN Update).

**Chicago's own Vision Zero** program (launched June 2017 under Mayor Emanuel, three-year action plan) uses the analogous **High Crash Corridors Framework** (June 2018), identifying 43 corridors covering 70 miles with disproportionately high severe-crash rates, and publishes raw, address-level crash data on the Chicago Data Portal beginning September 2017 (City of Chicago Vision Zero Annual Report 2017-18; High Crash Corridors Framework Plan).

**NACTO** (National Association of City Transportation Officials) publishes the **Urban Bikeway Design Guide** (3rd edition, 2025) — the FHWA-recognized reference standard cities cite when justifying facility types (protected/buffered/painted/none), directly mirroring the "report card" grading categories in the persona's world (NACTO Urban Bikeway Design Guide).

**League of American Bicyclists** runs the **Bicycle Friendly Community (BFC)** award and the **Benchmarking Report** (data.bikeleague.org), which merged BFC applications and benchmarking surveys because 48 of the 50 largest US cities already participate in both. It publishes 130+ tables/figures across 76 cities and 50 states on infrastructure, laws, and funding (League of American Bicyclists Benchmarking Report and Data Home).

**Active Transportation Alliance** (Chicago's own advocacy org) is the closest real-world analog to a dashboard user: it tracks Chicago's ~37 miles of protected lanes against 4,500+ miles of city streets, publishes an annual Chicago Regional Crash Report, and explicitly names "menu money" underfunding and aldermanic veto power as root causes of unequal bikeway distribution — Black and Brown South/West Side wards have fewer protected lanes despite higher crash rates (Active Transportation Alliance, "Moving beyond four main barriers to building protected bike lanes in Chicago").

**Bike Lane Uprising**, founded in Chicago by Christina Whitehouse, is the real organization behind the persona's "mock" obstruction layer — a crowdsourced app/database of bike-lane-blocking reports now active in 100+ cities, updated every 3 hours, used in litigation, legislative advocacy, and direct outreach to repeat-offender companies (Bike Lane Uprising, "About Us"; "How it works"; Streetsblog Chicago, 2018).

**ITDP** runs the global "Cycling Cities" campaign (70+ partner cities since 2021) and the **People Near Protected Bikelanes (PNB)** indicator for cross-city comparison of protected-network reach (ITDP, "How Does Cycling Infrastructure in These Global Cities Measure Up?").

## What data they actually use/publish/demand

- **LTS-classified street segments** (not just "has a bike lane" binary) — PeopleForBikes BNA.
- **Connectivity/reach scores** to specific destination categories (jobs, schools, groceries, parks, transit) rather than raw lane-mileage — PeopleForBikes BNA.
- **HIN corridor lists** built by fusing hospital trauma data + police crash reports to correct for the ~30-40% of severe injuries missing from police-only data — Vision Zero Network / SF DPH.
- **Facility-type taxonomies** (protected/buffered/conventional/sharrow/none) tied to design-guide standards — NACTO Urban Bikeway Design Guide.
- **Exposure-normalized safety metrics**: crash *rate* per rider-mile/rider-count, not raw crash counts — grounded in Jacobsen's "safety in numbers" finding that doubling ridership cuts per-cyclist crash rate by roughly a third, and in newer work using Strava/crowdsourced volume data (a 10% Strava-volume increase predicting only ~2% more crashes) to substitute for the chronic lack of real bicycle-count data (Utah signalized-intersection study, ScienceDirect 2025; Copenhagen exposure study).
- **Crowdsourced obstruction/violation reports** as a proxy layer where no official dataset exists — Bike Lane Uprising.
- **Menu-money/capital-spend line items per ward**, cross-referenced against crash and infrastructure gaps — Active Transportation Alliance, The Daily Line reporting on Chicago's $1.5M/ward discretionary "menu."
- **Legislative sponsorship tracking**: e.g., Ald. Andre Vasquez's 2023 "cyclists are permitted/intended users" ordinance (40+ co-sponsors) and the Smart Streets Pilot bike/bus-lane camera-enforcement ordinance co-sponsored by Alds. La Spata, Hopkins, Villegas, Vasquez, Reilly, and Martin (Illinois Answers Project, 2023; Streetsblog Chicago, 2023).

## Documented wins/failures where data played a role

- **Win**: SF's HIN methodology directly targeted capital investment and is credited by Vision Zero Network with strengthening multi-departmental coordination across 20+ adopting cities.
- **Failure/gap**: Chicago's aldermanic-prerogative system means CDOT largely installs bikeways only where the local alderman agrees, producing documented inequities — fewer protected lanes on South/West Side wards despite worse crash rates — an outcome advocacy groups attribute directly to how menu money is *not* tied to any objective danger index (Active Transportation Alliance).
- **Failure/gap**: Police-reported crash data is well-documented as severely undercounting cyclist injuries — international COST TU1101 survey found only ~10% average police-reporting rate; US estimates range 7-46% underreporting for bicyclists and 44-75% for pedestrians, with bias against less-severe, off-road, and non-motor-vehicle-involved crashes, and against Black and Hispanic victims specifically (COST TU1101 study; Smart Growth America; NHTSA/FARS literature).
- **Tension**: Vision Zero Network itself now publishes critiques of enforcement-based countermeasures, warning that camera/police enforcement (like Chicago's Smart Streets cameras) can reproduce racial bias in traffic stops even while marketed as data-driven safety tools (Vision Zero Network, "Vision Zero, Equity & Law Enforcement").

## What this implies for the persona

A person drawn from this world — an advocate, planner, journalist, or engaged rider — would talk fluently in **LTS/low-stress network**, **HIN**, **facility-type taxonomy**, and **exposure-normalized rates**, and would instinctively distrust any dashboard that shows raw crash counts or lane-miles without denominators (ridership, population, street-miles) or without acknowledging police-data undercounting. They would expect a "danger index" to be built HIN-style (multi-source, severity-weighted) rather than from a single feed, and would immediately ask whether a ward-level rollup controls for aldermanic menu-money discretion versus objective risk — because that's the documented local failure mode. They would recognize Bike Lane Uprising by name and be skeptical of "mock" data standing in for it, wanting clear provenance/refresh-cadence framing. They would likely be primed to probe equity questions (who's undercounted, whose wards get protected vs. painted lanes, whether camera-enforcement proxies encode bias) as a first-order concern, not an afterthought.

Note: I could not verify a specific PeopleForBikes/NACTO "danger index" product by that name, or a formal ITDP city index score for Chicago — these appear to be original constructs for this fictional dashboard, not real published indices, so the brief treats them as design choices to be interrogated against the real methodologies above rather than as pre-existing benchmarks to match exactly.

## Data-need signals (analyst-extracted)

- A dashboard needs crash-rate metrics normalized by ridership/exposure (e.g., per rider-mile or per estimated cyclist count), not raw crash counts, to match how PeopleForBikes and academic safety-in-numbers research actually measure risk.
- A dashboard's 'danger index' needs multi-source severity weighting (hospital + police-style fusion, akin to the San Francisco High Injury Network) rather than a single crash feed, given documented 30-75% underreporting of cyclist/pedestrian injuries in police data.
- Ward-level rollups need an explicit view of aldermanic menu-money spending versus objective risk/danger scores, since Chicago advocacy groups identify exactly this mismatch as the mechanism producing inequitable bike-lane distribution.
- Facility-type report cards need to use a recognizable standard taxonomy (protected/buffered/painted/none) traceable to NACTO's Urban Bikeway Design Guide so users can compare against national design norms.
- The bike-lane-obstruction layer needs clear provenance and refresh-cadence disclosure (mock vs. real Bike Lane Uprising feed) since the real Bike Lane Uprising app refreshes every ~3 hours and its credibility rests on transparent sourcing.
- A dashboard needs a way to flag which crash/complaint data plausibly undercounts non-white and lower-severity victims, given documented racial and severity bias in police-reported crash and enforcement data.
- A legislation-tracking view needs per-alderman sponsorship/co-sponsorship history (bill-level, not just yes/no support) to match how real Chicago ordinances (e.g., Vasquez's 2023 ordinance, Smart Streets Pilot) are actually reported and organized.
- A network-connectivity view needs destination-based reach scoring (jobs, schools, groceries, transit) in addition to raw lane-mileage, mirroring PeopleForBikes' BNA approach rather than treating 'miles of bike lane' as a sufficient metric.
