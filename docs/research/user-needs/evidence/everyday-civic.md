# Evidence Brief: The "Everyday Resident" Persona for a Chicago Bike-Safety Dashboard

## 1. Who the real actors are and how they operate

This persona is emphatically **not** a professional advocate. The professional/advocate layer around Chicago bike safety is well documented and gives us a contrast class: **Active Transportation Alliance** sends ward-by-ward crash-count letters to all 50 aldermanic offices and follows up personally with the local alderperson after every fatal bike/ped crash [Active Transportation Alliance](https://activetransportation.org/blog/the-ward-by-ward-effort-to-reduce-walking-biking-crashes/); **Streetsblog Chicago** and **Block Club Chicago** function as data-journalism intermediaries, regularly translating CDOT/CPD crash data, Cycling Strategy documents, and camera-ticket studies into narrative stories [Streetsblog Chicago](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2025/11/25/key-findings-from-cdots-annual-chicago-traffic-crashes-report), [Block Club Chicago](https://blockclubchicago.org/2025/09/23/speed-camera-program-should-axe-late-fees-boost-transparency-to-be-more-effective-report-says/); **Bike Lane Uprising**, founded by Christina Whitehouse after a near-miss with a truck, has crowdsourced 25,000+ Chicago obstruction reports over four years and used the data to push the Inspector General's office to add bike-lane enforcement to its 2021 Annual Plan [Streetsblog Chicago](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2020/10/29/the-new-bike-lane-uprising-app-makes-it-easier-than-ever-to-report-bikeway-obstructions), [Northwestern Magazine](https://magazine.northwestern.edu/people/christina-whitehouse-urban-cycling-safety-bike-lane-uprising-bike-lane-obstructions); and neighborhood-rooted groups like **Slow Roll Chicago** (South/West Side-focused rides, White House "Champion of Change" recognition in 2015) and **Better Streets Chicago** (founded 2020, explicitly organized to help ordinary residents "talk to their aldermen") sit between advocacy and grassroots organizing [Active Transportation Alliance](https://activetrans.org/blog/slow-roll-sees-bicycling-as-a-transformative-force/), [Streetsblog Chicago](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2020/05/14/better-streets-chicago-is-a-new-advocacy-group-with-a-pro-open-streets-agenda).

The persona we're grounding lives **downstream** of all this: someone who might read a Block Club article, glance at a Streetsblog map, maybe attend one ward night, but does not track CDOT PDFs or file FOIA requests. This "civic tech user gap" is itself a documented phenomenon — mySociety's cross-national research on civic technology users found participants skew older (70%+ over 45 in the US/UK), more educated, and disproportionately male ("male, pale and stale") [mySociety](https://www.mysociety.org/2015/10/28/who-benefits-from-civic-technology/), [Civic Innovations](https://civic.io/2015/10/29/who-uses-civic-tech/). Pew Research similarly finds local-meeting attendance (~29% of Americans in five years) is concentrated among higher-income, higher-education, older residents [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2013/04/25/civic-engagement-in-the-digital-age-2/). A persona meant to represent "everyday residents" should be explicitly positioned as someone civic-tech tools currently under-serve.

## 2. What data these actors actually use, publish, or demand

- **Crash data as currency**: CDOT's annual "Chicago Traffic Crashes" report and the city's Traffic Crashes open dataset are the backbone of nearly every advocacy claim — e.g., fatal crashes fell 30% citywide 2021–2024, but 72% of 2024 fatalities involved speeding, and cyclist crashes rose in 2023–2025 alongside network expansion [Streetsblog Chicago](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2025/11/25/key-findings-from-cdots-annual-chicago-traffic-crashes-report), [Active Transportation Alliance](https://activetransportation.org/blog/the-ward-by-ward-effort-to-reduce-walking-biking-crashes/).
- **Geographic/equity overlays**: Advocates consistently pair crash data with race/income maps — West Side (Austin, Garfield Park, North Lawndale) and Greater Grand Crossing show the highest concentration of severe crashes and historically the sparsest bike infrastructure [Streetsblog Chicago](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2024/10/24/common-sense-cdots-chicago-traffic-crashes-report-illustrates-the-effectiveness-of-bike-ped-investment), [Governing](https://www.governing.com/community/study-finds-biking-inequities-in-chicago-neighborhoods).
- **Camera/enforcement data as a fairness flashpoint**: ProPublica and academic studies (Sutton & Tilahun, UIC) found Chicago's "race-neutral" speed/red-light cameras disproportionately ticket Black and Latino drivers — households in Black/Latino ZIP codes get camera tickets at roughly twice the rate of white ZIP codes, and low-income residents incur late fees on 46% of tickets vs. 17% for higher-income residents [ProPublica](https://www.propublica.org/article/chicagos-race-neutral-traffic-cameras-ticket-black-and-latino-drivers-the-most), [Fines and Fees Justice Center](https://finesandfeesjusticecenter.org/articles/red-light-and-speed-camera-analyzing-the-equity-and-efficacy-of-chicagos-automated-camera-enforcement-program/). This is directly relevant: a dashboard using camera-violation data as a "danger proxy" will land in a community that already associates cameras with punitive revenue extraction, not safety.
- **Crowdsourced obstruction data**: Bike Lane Uprising's app asks users for photos, license plates, and company names; this maps closely to the dashboard's "mock" obstruction layer and signals real resident appetite for "who's blocking my lane right now" reporting, plus real-world precedent for turning that data into fleet-safety training programs [Streetsblog Chicago](https://chi.streetsblog.org/2025/04/08/bike-lane-uprising-announces-new-bikeway-safety-training-program-for-fleet-drivers).
- **Aldermanic menu money**: Each alderperson controls ~$1.5M/year in discretionary capital funds; historically only a handful of wards (46th under Cappleman, later 1st/33rd/35th/39th/47th/49th) spent menu money on bike infrastructure, and some wards now run participatory budgeting to let residents vote on it [The Daily Line](https://www.thedailyline.com/aldermanic-menu-money-how_s-it-spent), [Ward Wise](https://www.wardwisechicago.org/). This is the single most concrete "what do I do about it" lever available to a resident — it explains why a persona would care about "which projects has my alderman funded" more than abstract citywide stats.

## 3. Documented wins/failures where data played a role

- **Win**: Bike Lane Uprising's crowdsourced data reached the Chicago OIG and got bike-lane enforcement written into its 2021 Annual Plan — evidence that resident-submitted, hyperlocal, photo-based reports can move city policy when aggregated [Northwestern Magazine](https://magazine.northwestern.edu/people/christina-whitehouse-urban-cycling-safety-bike-lane-uprising-bike-lane-obstructions).
- **Win**: CDOT's "neighborhood-driven" approach (working with Austin Coming Together, Garfield Park Community Council, North Lawndale Community Coordinating Council) tripled bikeway mileage in Belmont Cragin and North Lawndale in two years and cut people killed/seriously injured 39% in Austin/Garfield Park/North Lawndale 2021–2024 — suggesting trusted local intermediaries, not raw dashboards, are what convert data into infrastructure [Active Transportation Alliance](https://activetransportation.org/blog/chicago-west-southwest-side/).
- **Failure/ongoing tension**: Despite crash data clearly showing West/South Side severity concentration, infrastructure investment has historically lagged there — a textbook case of data existing but not translating into equitable action without political/community pressure [Governing](https://www.governing.com/community/study-finds-biking-inequities-in-chicago-neighborhoods).
- **Failure/backlash risk**: the camera-enforcement equity research shows that data-driven "objective" safety tools can be experienced as punitive and unfair by exactly the communities a safety dashboard wants to serve — a caution against presenting a "danger index" or violation counts without acknowledging enforcement-equity concerns.

## 4. Data-quality and reporting-bias research (why raw dashboards mislead)

Multiple peer-reviewed studies (Kontokosta, Hong & Korsberg on NYC 311, ~20M requests plus building-violation and Census data; a parallel Chicago study; Boston research) consistently find that **311-style resident-reported data undercounts problems in exactly the neighborhoods that need help most**: areas with more unmarried, minority, unemployed, and limited-English-proficiency residents report at systematically lower rates, even controlling for physical conditions, while whiter, higher-income, higher-rent areas over-report [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.02452), [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2210670720307216), [Marron Institute/NYU](https://marroninstitute.nyu.edu/blog/equity-in-311-reporting). This is a direct, load-bearing fact for a dashboard that uses "311 bike complaints" as a danger proxy: the absence of complaints in a ward is not evidence of safety, and a persona from an under-reporting neighborhood would (correctly) distrust a map that reads that way. It also explains why open-data portals in general go underused by ordinary people — usability research finds portals are usable mainly by domain experts, not the public, and stale or incomplete data further erodes trust [ResearchGate usability studies](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/366218759_DESIGNING_FOR_USABILITY_OPEN_DATA_AFFORDANCES_AND_IMPACTS_ON_OPEN_DATA_USE).

## 5. Plain language, mobile-first, and intermediary patterns

Federal (Plain Writing Act of 2010) and City of Chicago (Mayor's Office for People with Disabilities plain-language guide) standards call for 6th–8th grade reading level for public materials, with even simpler "Easy Read" (3rd–5th grade) for accessibility-critical content [Digital.gov](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/media/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf), [Chicago MOPD](https://www.chicago.gov/content/dam/city/depts/mopd/supp_info/accessibility_guide_tools/communication_access/Plain%20Language%20Tips.pdf). Pew Research documents that over a quarter of adults in households under $30K income are "smartphone-only" internet users, making mobile-first design a genuine access requirement, not a nicety, for reaching lower-income residents on the West/South Sides who are exactly the population most affected by crash disparities [Pew via Cook + Schmid](https://www.cookandschmid.com/blog/mobile-phones-the-gateway-to-effective-public-engagement-in-low-income-communities/). Finally, the "data intermediary" literature (grassroots open-data intermediaries research; journalism-as-translation studies) confirms that ordinary residents rarely engage raw datasets directly — they receive data pre-digested by journalists (Streetsblog, Block Club), CSOs, or block clubs, who add narrative and local context [SAGE Journals](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/2053951717690750), [ACM Digital Government](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3585537). A persona should therefore plausibly say they heard about a crash/issue "from Block Club" or "someone posted it in the neighborhood Facebook group," not "I looked it up on the portal."

## 6. Implications for the persona

**Vocabulary**: "my ward," "my alderman," "menu money," "the corner by [landmark]," "I saw it on Block Club/the neighborhood group" — not "danger index," "KPI," or dataset names.
**Interests**: personal/family route safety (school run, commute), not citywide trends; whether their specific complaint/pothole/obstruction got fixed; whether their alderman is "one of the ones who spends on bike lanes."
**Frustrations**: distrust of camera/ticket data as revenue extraction rather than safety; skepticism that reporting something (311, obstruction app) actually leads to action; portals/dashboards that feel built for "data people."
**Latent/unstated needs**: hyper-local relevance (their own route, not the whole city) surfaced automatically rather than searched for; a clear, low-effort "what do I do" action (who to email, what to say, participatory-budgeting deadlines) rather than more charts; visible acknowledgment that missing data (311/complaint gaps) reflects reporting bias, not actual safety, especially for South/West Side users who would otherwise read a sparse map as "nothing's wrong here."

## Data-need signals (analyst-extracted)

- The dashboard should visually flag wards/areas where 311 bike-complaint counts are likely undercounts due to documented reporting bias, not present sparse complaint data as evidence of safety.
- Route-level, address-searchable views (my commute, my kid's school route) are needed more than citywide aggregate charts, since personal relevance drives engagement far more than systemic data.
- A direct 'what do I do' action layer is needed per ward — alderman contact info, current menu-money bike spending, and upcoming participatory-budgeting or ward-night dates — not just visualization.
- Any content should be written at roughly 6th-8th grade reading level per federal/Chicago plain-language standards, avoiding dataset jargon like 'danger index' or 'KPI' in resident-facing copy.
- The dashboard must be mobile-first and fast on low-end connections, since lower-income residents are disproportionately smartphone-only internet users.
- Speed/red-light camera violation data used as a safety proxy should be paired with equity context (documented racial/income disparities in ticketing) to avoid the tool reading as punitive or untrustworthy.
- A shareable, plain-language 'digest' or embeddable widget would let intermediaries (Block Club, neighborhood Facebook groups, block clubs) redistribute dashboard findings, matching how residents actually receive civic data secondhand.
- The mock bike-lane-obstruction layer should support the same crowdsourced, photo/plate-based reporting pattern as Bike Lane Uprising, since that is the model residents are already primed to expect and trust.
