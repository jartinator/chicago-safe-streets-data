# Interview: chi-community-organizer

## Round 1 transcript


**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time, Andre. Let's start with who you are when you're touching this kind of data. Can you describe your role, and walk me through the last decision you tried to influence about street safety — step by step?

**chi-community-organizer:** Sure. I coordinate the block-club coalition on the west end of Austin — we're talking maybe fourteen block clubs between Central and Austin Boulevard, north of the Ike. I sit on the CDOT task force for the neighborhood bike network, the one that came out of working with Austin Coming Together and some folks from Garfield Park. That's my volunteer hat, but it eats more hours than my actual job some months.

Last real decision — this spring, we were fighting to get Chicago Avenue between Central and Laramie onto this year's menu-money list for a protected lane extension, not just paint. Step by step: it starts with a resident, Miss Ella on the 5600 block, getting doored getting off the bus stop — no, sorry, getting doored while she was walking her bike across, car door opened right into her. She's fine, banged up. That goes into our block club WhatsApp, somebody takes a picture of the intersection, no markings, no protection. That becomes an agenda item at our monthly coalition meeting. I pull together whatever crash numbers I can get for that stretch — I go to the city's crash portal, I download what I can, I cross-reference against what Active Trans sent the alderman's office in their ward letter last year. Then I draft a one-pager, I bring it to the alderman's community affairs liaison — not the alderman herself first, you go through the liaison — and I ask for it to be raised with CDOT before the July menu deadline. Then it's a waiting game and a lot of follow-up calls.

**Interviewer:** Who do you need to convince in that process, and what does "convinced" look like for them?

**chi-community-organizer:** Three different people, three different bars. The alderman's office needs to feel like it's not just me and three block captains — they need to see it's the community asking, not Andre's pet project. So "convinced" for them looks like turnout: fifteen, twenty people at a ward night saying the same thing. CDOT's project engineer needs something closer to what they'd call engineering justification — crash history, maybe a petition, maybe photos of near-misses, because they've got a hundred corridors competing for the same dollars. And honestly, the third person I have to convince is my own neighbors, because half of them think anything with "bike lane" in the name is the city taking away parking to please people who don't even live here. That's a room I've stood in front of plenty of times.

**Interviewer:** What information sources do you touch in a normal week for this work?

**chi-community-organizer:** Block Club Chicago's Austin coverage, when they cover us, which isn't as much as I'd like. Text threads with my block captains — that's honestly my main dataset, people telling me what they saw. CDOT sends the task force an email update maybe once a month, sometimes it's just a PDF with a map nobody can read on a phone. If Active Trans has put out their ward letter that year I'll skim it for our numbers. And every few months I end up on the city's data portal trying to pull something myself, which — I'll be honest — I am not fast at. I know enough to filter by date and location if somebody walks me through it, but I've called my nephew more than once to help me get a spreadsheet to do what I want.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time you needed a number, map, or chart about bike safety specifically. Where did you go, what did you do with it, how long did it take?

**chi-community-organizer:** That Chicago Avenue one-pager I mentioned — that took me the better part of a Saturday. I went to the city's crash data site, filtered as best I could for that corridor, exported it, and then I had to manually go count how many of those were actually on Chicago Ave versus just nearby, because the filter wasn't precise the way I needed. Then I opened it in Excel and just eyeballed dooring incidents because I didn't trust the flag — I've heard dooring doesn't even get coded right half the time, officers write it up as something else. So I read every row description myself. Four hours, easy, for what ended up being a page and a half.

**Interviewer:** What have you built yourself — spreadsheets, screenshots, FOIAs — because nothing gave it to you directly?

**chi-community-organizer:** I've got a running spreadsheet — ugly thing, I built it in Google Sheets — of every speed hump and crossing request that's come through our coalition since 2022, with the date we asked, who we talked to, and whether anything happened. That's my receipts file. Half the value of it is just being able to tell CDOT "you told us this in March 2023, it's now been two years." I've also got a folder of screenshots — near misses, blocked lanes, a car parked dead in the bike lane on Lake Street for three days that somebody photographed every morning like a countdown. And yes, I've done a FOIA. Not me personally — Miss Denise, who used to work downtown, helped us FOIA 311 response times for our ward because the alderman's office kept saying things "were in the queue" and we wanted to know what queue meant. Took like ten weeks to get an answer.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a time data — or the lack of it — sank an argument you were making.

**chi-community-organizer:** This one still gets me. Two years back we were pushing for a protected extension near Austin and Chicago, and somebody from the budget side — not even CDOT, somebody adjacent, at a downtown meeting — said, and I remember this almost word for word, "we don't see the community demand here, 311 complaints for that segment are basically flat." And I sat there and I could not, in that room, prove to them that flat 311 numbers from Austin do not mean flat problems. It means people don't call 311 on us the way they do on the North Side — folks don't trust it does anything, some folks don't want police-adjacent city systems in their business at all, some folks just don't have the fifteen minutes. I knew that in my bones and I did not have a chart to put next to their chart. That's the day I decided I needed better ammunition than a feeling.

**Interviewer:** What data do you distrust, and what earned that distrust?

**chi-community-organizer:** Camera ticket numbers, flat out. Every time somebody tries to use red-light or speed camera data as a stand-in for "how dangerous is this street," I stop them, because I know good and well those cameras sit heavier on our side of town and ticket us at a higher clip than they ticket over east, and the late fees land on people who can least afford them. That's not a safety measure to me, that's a collection machine with a badge on it. And I'll say it plain: 311 counts, same story, different reason — I don't trust a sparse 311 map to mean a safe block, I trust it to mean nobody with a working phone and forty-five minutes wanted to sit on hold.

**Interviewer:** Let's walk through what a site called "On Your Left" actually offers — an independent, open-source dashboard on Chicago bike safety. I'll go screen by screen. First: there's a Leaflet-style map showing crash density, the bikeway network color-coded by facility type, ward boundaries, camera locations, and a mock layer of bike-lane obstructions, all overlaid.

**chi-community-organizer:** Okay, first question out of my mouth is always the same — what's this going to make Austin look like, and who's looking at it? If it's crash dots stacked up thick on my side of the map with no bikeway lines next to them, that's either the exact chart I need for the task force, or it's a picture some reporter downtown uses to write "Austin: most dangerous for cyclists" with zero context about why — no lanes, no protection, decades of nothing. Same numbers, opposite use, depending who's holding the mic. I want to know: does this thing explain *why*, or does it just leave the dots there for people to draw their own conclusions?

And the camera locations sitting right there on the same map as crash data — no. I don't want those on the same layer as "safety." That tells people the cameras are protecting them. I know what those cameras are for on our side of town, and it isn't protecting bikers.

**Interviewer:** Fair — and just so it's clear, the obstruction layer on that map is explicitly labeled mock, entirely synthetic, not real reports.

**chi-community-organizer:** Then I'm not using it. Not in a church basement, not anywhere. I don't care that it's labeled — half the room isn't going to read the fine print on a badge, they're going to see a map with dots on their block and think that's real. I've spent years trying to get people to trust that when I bring them a number, it's a real number. I'm not putting made-up data in front of my neighbors and hoping the asterisk holds up. If you want an obstruction layer, get the real Bike Lane Uprising numbers or don't show me a map at all.

**Interviewer:** Understood. There's also a second map — a schematic, subway-map-style view of twenty-one named bike routes, each one solid-colored end to end, with an optional toggle showing an infrastructure-quality border around each line.

**chi-community-organizer:** So this one's showing me — wait, is this ranking the routes by how good they are? Because if a route's got that colored border going all the way around it, my first read is that's the route the city's invested in, this is basically a report card for which corridors got taken care of. That's useful to me right off — I want to see if any of "our" routes even made the twenty-one.

**Interviewer:** Just to clarify — that page doesn't carry any crash or safety data at all. The border is a quality indicator, separate page from the safety map.

**chi-community-organizer:** See, that's exactly the kind of split that loses people. You've got the safety information on one page and the "here's how nice the route looks" on another page, and nobody in my meetings is going to click through two maps to connect them. I want one place that tells me: this route, this is how many people got hurt on it, this is what it's actually built out of. Splitting it makes it feel like two different products for two different audiences — one for planners, one for us.

**Interviewer:** Next, the Findings page — curated cards, each with a headline stat, a caveat, and a link into the map. Things like citywide KSI trend, percent of the network protected, street coverage, top corridors, hit-and-run rate, ward concentration, and a card specifically about dooring being undercounted.

**chi-community-organizer:** Now that dooring card — I'd screenshot that tomorrow. That's the one I've been trying to say for years without a source behind me: what happened to Miss Ella doesn't even show up right in the official numbers because dooring gets miscoded or dropped depending how it's reported. If there's a card that says that in plain language with a source, I'm printing that and bringing it to the next CDOT sit-down. Same with ward concentration, if it shows Austin and Garfield Park and Lawndale stacked up — that confirms what we already knew and helped fix through the task force, so that's a receipt, not a surprise.

**Interviewer:** There's also a sortable ward table with a CSV export — including new columns for percent of the ward's bikeway network that's protected, and percent of streets with any bike infrastructure.

**chi-community-organizer:** That I'd actually download. I'd drop those numbers straight into my own spreadsheet next to my speed-hump tracker. My worry is the same one from the map, though — if there's some kind of overall danger score column sitting next to it —

**Interviewer:** There is — a 0-to-100 comparable safety index, blending crashes per population and crashes per bikeway mile.

**chi-community-organizer:** Right, and my honest first instinct looking at a number like that — I'd probably assume higher number means safer, like a grade, ninety-out-of-a-hundred is good. So somebody's going to need to tell me flat out which way that number runs, because if Austin comes back as a ninety and it means "ninety percent as dangerous as it gets," and I read that as a good grade in front of a room, that's a bad night for me. That's the kind of number that gets ammunition-or-stigma real fast depending who explains it. I'd want it dead simple, worse-color-means-worse, before I'd ever hold it up.

**Interviewer:** Moving to accountability data — council legislation on bike and street safety, sponsorship counts and recorded no-votes per alderman, ward-office contact info, and upcoming Pedestrian and Traffic Safety Committee hearings.

**chi-community-organizer:** Now we're talking. This is the stuff I actually need. If I can walk into a coalition meeting and say "here's how many bike-safety ordinances your alderman actually sponsored, here's the ones she voted no on," that is a mic moment. That's "who's at the table" made visible. Hearing dates — yes, that's how I get twelve people on a bus downtown to sit in those blue chairs and make an alderman uncomfortable in public. That part of the site, I don't even need convincing on.

**Interviewer:** And menu-money spending — an extract from an independent civic-tech group tracking how much of each ward's discretionary capital funds went to bike safety, though the site notes it hasn't been verified against the original source PDFs.

**chi-community-organizer:** That "not verified" line matters more than you'd think. Menu money is the thing — that's the whole reparative-investment argument, did they actually spend the money on us or did it go to repaving the same three blocks by the alderman's house again. But if I bring an unverified number into a room and somebody's cousin works in the ward office and says "that's wrong," I lose the room and I don't get it back. I'd use it to point myself toward the right question, but before it goes in front of people I'm asking somebody who knows the actual PDFs to check it first. I'm not going in front of my community with a maybe.

**Interviewer:** Let's do the gap probing now. Picture this: you're at a coalition meeting in the basement of a church on Central Avenue, after another close call, and residents are openly saying they don't trust the city's numbers — "they always get our numbers wrong," somebody says. You pull up OYL. What do you look for first, and where does it fail you?

**chi-community-organizer:** First thing I look for is our block, our corridor — did this thing happen where somebody's saying it happened, is there a crash on record near there, does the dooring undercount note apply. And here's where it fails me fast: there's no way to just type in an address or even a cross street and get an answer. I've got to know I'm going ward, then corridor, then intersection, and half my room doesn't know what ward they're in, and I'm not going to be scrolling a desktop map on my phone in a basement with bad wifi trying to zoom into the right block while twenty people wait. I need something I can print before I walk in the door, not something I drive live.

And the bigger failure — there's nothing on there that shows me the money and the race and income side by side with the crashes. I can pull crash concentration from this site, but if I want to show "look, this is the same map as the poorest blocks in the ward," I've still got to go get that myself from somewhere else and lay it on top by hand. That's the piece that actually wins the argument in my rooms — not just "it's dangerous here," but "it's dangerous here *and* here's why nobody fixed it."

**Interviewer:** Of everything OYL doesn't have, which single absence costs you the most?

**chi-community-organizer:** That one — no equity layer. No race, no income, nothing that lets me show the disinvestment pattern in the same place as the danger pattern. Without that it's just a map of where people got hurt, and a map like that on its own, in the wrong hands, reads like "Austin's a war zone," not "Austin got skipped for forty years and here's the receipt."

**Interviewer:** If OYL handed you one export every week, what would it contain, and who would you send it to?

**chi-community-organizer:** Crashes and near-miss trend for our specific corridors, the ward's menu-money spend broken out by category, and whatever's newly on the hearing calendar. One page. I'd send it to my block captains group text and to the alderman's community liaison, same person, every week, so it stops being "Andre's opinion" and starts being "here's what's on the record again this week."

**Interviewer:** The site refuses to normalize crash counts by ridership volume — no counters, no bikeshare data — and says so plainly. Does that caveat change how you'd use the numbers? What would you accept as good-enough exposure data?

**chi-community-organizer:** Honestly, it doesn't change much for me, because I already do that math in my head — I know Austin's got fewer people riding than Lincoln Park, partly *because* there's nowhere safe to ride, so raw crash counts already undersell how dangerous it is per rider out here. I wouldn't trust something like Strava data for that anyway — that's going to be whoever's got the app and the confidence to ride without protection, which is not most of my neighbors. What I'd actually trust is something like what we did for the task force — actual counts CDOT or the task force took standing on a corner, or resident surveys asking who rides and where. Somebody with a clipboard, not an app.

**Interviewer:** Last section — magic wand. One dataset that doesn't publicly exist appears, clean and current. What is it?

**chi-community-organizer:** Menu money spent on bike and pedestrian safety, by ward, by year, next to crash severity by ward, by year, going back to when the money program started — so I can show, plainly, which wards got the investment and which wards got the danger. That's the whole argument in one table. Reparative investment made visible.

**Interviewer:** One chart or map you could put on a screen in front of your hardest audience that ends an argument?

**chi-community-organizer:** Two lines, side by side, same wards down the left: bar for severe crashes, bar for bikeway miles built, over the same years — and Austin's severe-crash bar tall and the miles bar short, next to some North Side ward where it's flipped. No index, no fancy score, just the two plain numbers next to each other. You don't need me to explain that chart. It explains itself.

**Interviewer:** And what would make you stop using a site like this after trying it once?

**chi-community-organizer:** If I catch that mock obstruction data getting cited somewhere as if it's real — once that trust breaks, I'm done, and I tell everybody I know not to use it either. If it goes stale — I bring a number to a hearing and CDOT's got a fresher one and mine's from eight months back, that's a credibility hit I can't afford twice. And if it stays a desktop tool that nobody can hand a printout from or pull up on a cracked phone screen in a basement, I'll keep using my own spreadsheet, same as I do now, and this just becomes one more thing built *about* us instead of *for* us.

## Follow-up round

**Interviewer:** You mentioned calling your nephew more than once to get a spreadsheet on the city's data portal to "do what you want" — what specifically is he doing in that moment? Is it the filtering, the export format, joining it to your corridor, or something else you can't do yourself on a phone or laptop?

**Andre:** My nephew Marcus, he's twenty-six, does something with logistics software downtown, and I call him and I put him on speaker while I've got the laptop open, and it's almost never the filtering part — I can click date ranges, I can figure out a dropdown. It's two other things. One, the export dumps everything as this raw CSV with column headers that don't mean anything to a regular person — like the location field isn't "Chicago and Central," it's some coordinate string, or a block range that doesn't match how I think about the corridor. Marcus knows how to write whatever they call it, a formula, to pull just the rows where the address falls between Central and Laramie, because Chicago Avenue in the data might list forty different exact addresses and I need to know which forty. Two, once I've got the right rows, I want it to look like something — a little bar chart, dates on one side — and I don't know Excel well enough to make a chart come out looking like it wasn't made by a fifty-two-year-old on his lunch break. So it's cleaning up my filter and then dressing it up so I'm not embarrassed to hand it to CDOT. Twenty minutes with him on the phone does what would take me the rest of a Saturday alone.

**Interviewer:** You called the speed-hump-and-crossing spreadsheet your "receipts file." Walk me through the last time you actually opened it in front of CDOT or the alderman's office — what did you point to, and would you want that history hosted anywhere other than your own Google Sheet, or does it need to stay yours?

**Andre:** Last time was a task force call in April. Somebody from CDOT's planning side was walking through this year's crossing improvement list and Austin wasn't on it, and I pulled the sheet up and said "Central and Iowa — we asked for that crossing in September 2022, you told us it was under review, it's now been two years and seven months, can somebody tell me what 'under review' means." That row, date, who I talked to, their exact words if I wrote them down — that shuts down the "we're still looking into it" answer real quick because now it's not a feeling, it's a paper trail with their own words in it.

As for hosting it somewhere else — no, and I'll tell you why. That sheet only works because it's mine. Nobody edited it but me and maybe one co-chair, so when I hold it up nobody can say "well, somebody typo'd that" or "who added that row." The minute it lives on some outside platform that other people can touch, or that OYL or anybody else controls, I lose the thing that makes it credible, which is that it's a plain record I built with my own hands and I can defend every line of it. Now — if there was a place I could export my own rows *into*, alongside the official data, so I could see them side by side without losing ownership of my sheet, I might look at that. But my receipts stay my receipts.

**Interviewer:** The photos of the car parked in the Lake Street bike lane for three days, taken like a countdown — what happened to those after you took them? Did they go into a 311 report, an email to the alderman, social media, or just the folder? Would a place to log dated photo evidence against a specific block matter to you, or is a folder on your phone already good enough?

**Andre:** Those went three places. First morning, one of my block captains posted it to our neighborhood Facebook group just venting. Second morning I said, no, let's actually do something with this, so I had her 311 it — for whatever that's worth, which per our earlier conversation isn't much, but you still do it for the record. Third morning I forwarded all three photos, dated, in one email to the community affairs liaison with the subject line "same car, day 3, Lake and Latrobe" because I wanted it in writing that this wasn't a one-time thing, this was neglect. Car finally got towed on day four, and I don't know if it was my email or just a beat cop having a good morning, honestly.

Now, would a place to log that matter to me? Some. Right now it's a phone folder named "bike lane stuff" that's a mess — I've got things from three different corridors mixed in there and I have to scroll to find what I need. If there was somewhere I could drop a photo, a date, and a cross street and it just sat there organized and I could pull "everything on Lake Street since January," yeah, I'd use that instead of scrolling. But it can't be one more login I have to remember, and it can't be public by default — some of what I document I'm not ready to hand the world, I'm building a case first.

**Interviewer:** Miss Denise's FOIA on 311 response times took ten weeks to come back. Once you had the answer, what did you actually do with it — did it change the "it's in the queue" conversation with the alderman's office, or did the ten-week wait mean the moment had passed?

**Andre:** Both, honestly, which is the frustrating part. By the time it came back the specific pothole-and-crossing complaints we'd started the FOIA over had mostly gotten handled one way or another, other stuff had moved on, people forget what started it. But the numbers themselves were still useful — turned out our ward's average response time on infrastructure-type 311 requests was something like double what a North Side ward we compared it to was running. So I didn't get to use it as "fix this specific thing," I got to use it as "stop telling us it's a queue like everybody's treated the same, because here's the math showing we wait twice as long." That went into a coalition meeting a couple months later as a standing fact I now just quote — I don't pull the actual FOIA document out anymore, I just say it, because ten weeks taught me you FOIA for the pattern, not for the emergency. If I need something fast, FOIA is the wrong tool, full stop — that's a slow-drip weapon, not a same-week one.

**Interviewer:** On the camera layer: if OYL kept camera locations but moved them to a completely separate page with zero safety framing, would that resolve it for you — or do you not want camera location data associated with OYL at all?

**Andre:** Separate page helps some, I won't pretend it doesn't — at least then nobody's eyeballing a map and reading "camera equals protection" into it. But I'd still want to know why it's on there at all, what OYL thinks I'd use it for. If it's framed as "here's where enforcement revenue gets collected, here's the ticket disparity by ward," now you're telling the same story I already know and I'd actually use that page — that's an accountability page, same family as your council-voting stuff. But if it's just a neutral little pin layer with no framing, no ticket counts, no disparity numbers next to it, then it's just decoration that somebody downtown could screenshot without context and use to say "look, cameras everywhere in Austin, they're so protected." So — move it, yes, but don't leave it empty. Put the receipts on that page too, or don't bother putting it up.

**Interviewer:** Is a literal paper printout the actual requirement, or would a page that reliably works offline or on weak signal solve the same problem — and if it's paper, does it need to be one page, or is a short packet fine?

**Andre:** Paper's the requirement for the room, not out of stubbornness — it's because I've stood in that church basement on Central where the wifi is one bar if you're lucky, and I am not going to be the guy fumbling a loading spinner in front of thirty people who are already halfway to not trusting the number. If I've got it printed, it works every single time, full stop, no excuses. Something that worked offline on my phone — cached, doesn't need signal — that'd cover me for the smaller stuff, following up with an individual or checking something mid-conversation. But for the actual meeting, the actual mic moment, I want paper in my hand or up on a poster board, because paper doesn't buffer.

And it doesn't have to be one page for everything — my Chicago Avenue one-pager was one page on purpose, that's the leave-behind, that's what goes in somebody's hand at a ward night. But I'll bring a short packet, three, four pages, stapled, to the actual sit-down with CDOT's engineer, because that room wants the backup data behind the headline. So: one page to persuade a room, a short packet to survive a Q&A. Both matter, they're for different doors.
