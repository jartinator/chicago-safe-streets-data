# Interview: nl-network-planner

## Round 1 transcript

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time — I know it's evening for you in Utrecht. Let's start simple. Can you describe your role, and walk me through the last decision you tried to influence about street safety?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Evening, yes, but this is a good hour for me, after dinner, before the news. So — I am an independent adviser now. Fourteen years at the Fietsersbond branch here, and since 2019 I do project work: network plans, and I run BYPAD audits, the policy audit, for municipalities that want to know where they stand.

The last one — I finished a module in April for a mid-size municipality, I won't say which, they haven't published yet, that's not mine to give away. But the shape of it: the wethouder for mobility — that's the alderman, roughly, but one person, not fifty like you have — wanted a cycling-safety paragraph for the coalition agreement. My job in the BYPAD process was to bring the planners, two police traffic officers, and three cyclist representatives from the local Fietsersbond branch into one room, four sessions, and score the municipality across the nine modules. Cohesion of the network was the weak module. Their own engineers, when I asked them to draw missing links on a blank map, drew almost the same map three different times independently — a gap on the ring road near the station, a gap on the industrial side. That convergence is what convinced the wethouder, not any single crash number. Three professionals independently drawing the same hole in the network is more persuasive to a Dutch official than a spreadsheet.

**Interviewer:** Who do you need to convince in that kind of work, and what does "convinced" look like for them?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Wethouders, mostly, and sometimes the gemeenteraad — the council — if it's contentious, which is rare here compared to what I gather Chicago is like. "Convinced" for a wethouder means: a number they can put in a coalition agreement with a year attached. Copenhagen's Bicycle Account does this — 80% satisfaction with cycle-track width by 2025, that kind of thing. If I bring a wethouder a finding without a target-year, they hand it back to me and say, "so what do I promise people." I've had that literally happen, a wethouder named Van der Berg — not real name, obviously, this is how these conversations go — pushed a directness-index number back across the table in 2022 and said, "this is a fact, not a policy." He wanted the target attached before he'd use it publicly.

**Interviewer:** What information sources do you touch in a normal week?

**Marloes van Dijk:** SWOV's fact sheets for national crash trends — that's our authoritative body, like your NHTSA I suppose, except we trust it, it's not political. CROW's manual, which I still open more than I'd like to admit, even after decades — the network principles, cohesion, directness, safety, comfort, attractiveness, in that order of my attention usually. Fietsbalans data when I'm scoring an older client against its own history. And now, since I started hosting delegations, some American materials — study-tour packets, mostly from your side, so I can prepare answers to questions I know are coming, like "what's the speed limit here" as if that's the whole story.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the last time you needed a number, map, or chart about bike safety, and what you did to get it.

**Marloes van Dijk:** Two months ago. I was preparing a slide for a masterclass — we host these for the Dutch Cycling Embassy, groups of foreign officials come through Utrecht for a week. I wanted to show cyclist satisfaction trend against infrastructure investment, side by side, for a Danish comparison slide, because Copenhagen's numbers are the cleanest political story we have. I went to the Bicycle Account PDF directly — 2022 edition — because there's no live dashboard for that, you download the biennial report like a document, not a database. I pulled the 80%/55%/47%/68% satisfaction figures by hand into a spreadsheet, cross-referenced against the 2016 edition for the trend line, and built the chart myself in maybe forty minutes. That's normal for me. Nothing in my world is one click.

**Interviewer:** What have you built yourself because nothing gave it to you directly?

**Marloes van Dijk:** A missing-link tracker, informally — a shared spreadsheet I keep updating client to client, generic categories of gap types I've seen: ring-road crossings, industrial-zone gaps, school-zone approach gaps. It's pattern recognition across projects, not official data, just my own accumulated notes, but I use it in every intake session now because it saves an afternoon of mapping from scratch. And for Fietsbalans-adjacent work where a client wants informal benchmarking against a neighbor town that hasn't done the full audit, I've built a rough proxy scorecard using whatever public numbers exist — crash rates, some open infrastructure length data — but I always caveat it heavily, because it isn't the real methodology, it's my substitute when the real one costs more than the client wants to spend.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about a time data — or the lack of it — sank an argument you were making.

**Marloes van Dijk:** The 's-Hertogenbosch-style case is the positive version — crash counts alone moved that redesign. But I had the opposite happen in 2023. I was arguing for a network-level intervention, not a single intersection, at a town I'll call by initial only, D. I brought crash counts for a corridor. The traffic engineer on the other side, not hostile, just careful, asked me: "compared to how many riders use that corridor versus the quiet one three streets over?" I didn't have volume counts for either street. I had counts from two years ago for one of them from an old temporary tube counter, nothing for the other. My argument collapsed in the room because I was comparing an absolute number to nothing. I left and did not get the network intervention that year — got a single intersection fix instead, which is exactly the project-by-project outcome I was trying to avoid.

**Interviewer:** What data do you distrust, and what earned that?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Self-reported hazard data without a resolution loop. Our Meldpunt — the Fietsersbond hazard-reporting tool — I trust because I know how it's built: geotagged photo reports route to the actual responsible municipal department, and there's a status, resolved or not. A report with no accountable recipient and no closure status, to me, is just noise with a pin on a map. I got burned once trusting an early, informal citizen pothole spreadsheet a volunteer kept — turned out half the "unresolved" entries had actually been fixed eighteen months earlier, nobody updated the sheet. I don't repeat that mistake. I ask "who owns this number, who updates it, and what happens when it's wrong" before I use anything now.

**Interviewer:** Let's look at what a Chicago project called "On Your Left" actually offers. It's an independent, open-source, read-only dashboard — doesn't accept reports itself, points people to the city's 311 system and a crowdsourced group called Bike Lane Uprising instead. Every data layer is tagged real, proxy, mock, crowdsourced, derived, or no-data-yet. I'll go screen by screen. First: there's a geographic map showing crash density, the bikeway network colored by facility type, wards, cameras, and obstruction "heat."

**Marloes van Dijk:** Facility type coloring, good, that's the first thing I look for on any network — I want to see protected versus painted versus sharrow at a glance, that's basic. But crash density on its own, layered visually next to infrastructure — this is the trap. It invites you to read "more crashes here" as "more dangerous here," and without riders-per-day underneath it, that reading is simply wrong. I'd want to know immediately: does darker red mean more crashes, or more crashes per cyclist? If I can't answer that in three seconds looking at the legend, I don't use the map in front of an audience. The cameras layer — what is that for, enforcement locations? I'd want that separated entirely from the safety story, that's a different policy conversation than infrastructure.

**Interviewer:** Second, there's a schematic "network map" — a transit-map style diagram, twenty-one named main routes each drawn in one solid color end-to-end, with interchange nodes, an optional quality-grade border you can toggle, and no safety data on that page at all.

**Marloes van Dijk:** [pause] Now — my first reaction, honestly, was relief. Finally, a page that thinks in routes and not dots. I was ready to say this is the closest thing to network thinking on the whole site. But wait — walk me through it again. Twenty-one *named* main routes, curated, and the quality border is optional? So this isn't derived from your actual street grid, cohesion gap by cohesion gap — this is a Chicago-invented ridership skeleton, a curated set of corridors somebody chose. I initially read it as "the network," full stop, the way I'd read a transit map as the actual system. It isn't. It's a roster. That's a real correction I need — I was about to praise this as directness-and-cohesion analysis and it's an editorial highlight reel of streets somebody thought mattered. Where are the twenty-two gaps I'd care about — the ones *between* those twenty-one lines? A schematic network map with no way to show a missing link is, to me, decoration, not planning data. I'd use it maybe as an orientation image for a slide, never as evidence.

**Interviewer:** Third — Findings, curated cards: KSI trend, protected-lane share, street coverage — 3,944 miles of surface street, 446 miles or 11% with any bike infrastructure — top corridors, hit-and-run, ward concentration, and a card flagging dooring as undercounted.

**Marloes van Dijk:** The coverage number, 11%, that's usable — that's the kind of single figure a wethouder equivalent could put in a headline, though I'd immediately ask for the Fietsbalans-style companion: coverage of *what standard*? Eleven percent against a named grid-spacing target means something; eleven percent alone is just a fact hanging in the air, the same complaint Van der Berg gave me in 2022. The dooring-undercount card — I respect that, that's honest, that's the kind of caveat SWOV would print, not hide. But "findings" as a genre — curated cards with a stat and a caveat — this is advocacy communication, not planning data. I'd use one or two of these cards in a masterclass slide to show Americans what transparency about data quality looks like. I would not build a network plan from a findings page. That's not what it's for, and I don't think it pretends to be.

**Interviewer:** Fourth, the ward table — sortable rankings, CSV export, now with percent-protected and percent-of-streets-with-bikeways columns.

**Marloes van Dijk:** This is the closest thing to Fietsbalans on the whole site, and it's the one I'd actually open a spreadsheet next to. Rankable, exportable, comparable ward to ward — that's benchmarking, that creates the pressure I described, a ward alderman seeing they rank 47th of 50 is the Dutch "spring into action" mechanism. But I'd want to know: is this ranking published as a defensible methodology anywhere, with the weighting explained, or is it just "here are three columns, sort as you like"? Fietsbalans works because the eleven criteria and their weights are public and defended. If I export this CSV and someone asks me "why is percent-protected weighted the way it implicitly is by being column three," I need an answer I can point to, a document, not "the dashboard just shows it."

**Interviewer:** There's also a ward safety index — a derived 0-to-100 score blending crashes per population and crashes per bikeway-mile, described explicitly as relative, not absolute risk.

**Marloes van Dijk:** Crashes per bikeway-mile — not crashes per rider. I want to be very direct about this, because it's the whole thing I keep circling back to. Bikeway-mile is infrastructure supply, not demand. A ward with three miles of protected lane and heavy ridership will look identical, on this index, to a ward with three miles of protected lane and almost nobody riding it, if their crash counts happen to match. That is not exposure. I would not present this number to a wethouder-equivalent as a risk score, full stop — I'd present it as what it says it is, relative, and I'd immediately ask the room "relative to what, and normalized by whom riding." If I used this uncritically in front of the D. town engineer who caught me in 2023, he'd catch this in the first minute, and I would deserve it.

**Interviewer:** Fifth — the council and alderman accountability layer: legislation records, an automated topic tag, sponsorship counts and recorded no-votes per alderman, committee hearing schedules, and a "menu money" spending proxy — that's a program where each of your fifty aldermen gets a discretionary infrastructure budget for their ward.

**Marloes van Dijk:** [laughs] This is where I become the curious outsider you warned me I'd be. Fifty individual politicians, each with a personal infrastructure budget, deciding block by block whether a bike lane happens in their ward? That is genuinely strange to me — it's the project-by-project instinct taken to its extreme, structurally, not just culturally. We do not have fifty small treasuries deciding network gaps street by street; national and provincial cycling money follows the Tour de Force targets, coordinated across cities. I don't fully know what to do with "menu money," and the site itself says it's not verified against the source documents, so I'd treat it the way I'd treat my own rough proxy scorecard — useful for a hunch, not for a claim. The sponsorship counts as a "safety record" — I'd want to be told plainly, is a sponsorship a vote? The evidence note says it's a proxy, not a tally, and honestly that distinction matters enormously to me and I suspect gets lost by anyone skimming fast. That's the kind of pairing I'd want — sponsorship *plus* before/after crash or ridership change per ward, so the accountability data actually measures an outcome, not just activity. As it stands, it measures who talked about the thing, not who changed the thing.

**Interviewer:** Sixth, the badge system itself — real, proxy, mock, crowdsourced, derived, no-data-yet — applied to every layer, including bike-lane obstructions which are labeled entirely mock, synthetic, pending a data-sharing conversation with Bike Lane Uprising.

**Marloes van Dijk:** The badge system, I want to say clearly, is the single most professionally respectable thing on this site. It's the discipline SWOV has and the discipline I demand of my own proxy scorecards. If more American tools did this, my job hosting delegations would be easier — half of what I correct in masterclasses is Americans presenting a scraped number as gospel.

But the mock obstruction layer — I need to say this plainly: I would never put synthetic data on a screen in front of a client, not once, not with a badge, not with a footnote. It doesn't matter how honestly it's labeled. A wethouder or an alderman does not read badges, their staff does maybe, but the moment a fabricated heat pattern is visually indistinguishable at a glance from a real one, someone will screenshot it into a deck without the caption, and now I've put fiction into a policy argument with my name near it. Our Meldpunt only works because every dot is a real photograph from a real person, routed to a real desk with a real resolution status. A synthetic obstruction layer, however clearly marked, is not a version of that I would touch. I'd rather have an honest blank page saying "no data yet" — which, I notice, is also one of your badges, and that one I respect completely.

**Interviewer:** Now the gap-probing section. Picture the scenario suited to you: you're advising a city — think of it as advising Chicago the way you'd advise a Dutch or Danish municipal client — on setting network-level cycling targets. You open OYL. What do you look for first, and where does it fail you?

**Marloes van Dijk:** First thing, always, in any advisory engagement: where is the network cohesion picture — the missing links, the detour factor, the grid-spacing gap against a stated standard. I go to the network map hoping for this, and I get the schematic instead, which as I said is a roster of named corridors, not a cohesion analysis. So already, in the first two minutes of an advisory engagement, I have to tell my hypothetical Chicago client: I cannot do a CROW-style network audit from what's here. I would need to build my own gap-map by hand, the way I did for the D. town engagement, overlay the bikeway segment file against the surface-street file myself and go hunting for holes — and even then I have no way to weight a gap by how many people would use it if it existed, because there's no demand model, no volume data anywhere in this stack.

Second thing I look for: satisfaction data, a rider's voice, anything resembling a survey. There is none. Not a badge for it, not a stub for it, it's simply absent from the inventory as a category. In my world this is not a nice-to-have, it's the thing that moved Copenhagen more than the crash statistics did. Its total absence here tells me this dashboard was built by people thinking about incidents, not about the felt experience of riding — which is precisely the "safety framed only as crashes" complaint I carry into every one of these conversations.

**Interviewer:** Of everything OYL does not have, what single absence costs you most?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Exposure. Ridership. Volume. I would say this in my sleep. Without knowing how many cyclists actually use a corridor, every crash number on this entire site — the ward index, the corridor cards, the density map — is describing where crashes happened to riders who happened to be there, not where cycling is dangerous. I cannot build a defensible network target — "reduce serious injuries per million cyclist-kilometers by X% by 2030" is the kind of sentence I'd want to write for a client — without a denominator. Right now every target I could write from this data would have to be phrased as raw counts, and a target phrased in raw counts can be hit simply by fewer people choosing to ride, which is the opposite of the outcome anyone wants. That's not a hypothetical — that's exactly the tension SWOV is wrestling with publicly right now, rising absolute cyclist deaths even as our own infrastructure improves, because ridership itself is rising faster. Chicago doesn't even have the counts to know if that tension exists for them yet.

**Interviewer:** If OYL handed you one export or artifact each week, what would it contain, and who would you forward it to?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Ward table CSV, honestly, with the coverage columns, forwarded to my own working file — I'd fold it into whatever comparative scorecard I'm building for a client that month. That's the one piece of this site with the shape of something I already do weekly. I wouldn't forward the findings cards or the network map to anyone; those are for their own audience, not something I'd stake my name on passing along.

**Interviewer:** The site refuses to normalize by ridership because it has no volume data, and says so plainly. Does that caveat change how you'd use the site? What would you accept as good-enough exposure data?

**Marloes van Dijk:** It changes everything about how I'd use it — it turns every crash number here from a safety statement into an incident log, and I'd say that to a client's face if I were presenting it. What would I accept as good-enough, not perfect? Honestly, not much is needed to start. Bikeshare trip counts alone, even without a full network of automatic counters, would let me do a rough per-corridor rate for the corridors bikeshare actually covers — better than nothing. A handful of visible, Copenhagen-style automatic counters at a dozen key corridors, refreshed even monthly, would be transformative, partly because of what they measure and partly because visible counters do something political — they make cycling volume legible to the public the same way a crash pin does, they're the "look how many of us there are" argument as much as a data point. I would not insist on perfect coverage before I'd start using it. I would insist on *some* denominator before I called anything a "danger index."

**Interviewer:** Last section. Magic wand — one dataset that doesn't publicly exist appears, clean and current. What is it?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Cyclist-kilometers by corridor, citywide, monthly. Not perfect count-post precision — I'd take modeled estimates from bikeshare plus a modest counter network plus household travel survey data, blended, the way some cities already do this imperfectly. That single dataset turns every other layer on this site from an incident map into an actual safety instrument. I keep saying this and I know I sound like a broken record, but that is genuinely the honest answer, it isn't rhetorical.

**Interviewer:** One chart or map you could put in front of your hardest audience that ends an argument. Describe it.

**Marloes van Dijk:** Two lines on one chart, over ten years: cyclist-kilometers ridden, and serious-injury rate *per* cyclist-kilometer, for the same corridor, side by side with infrastructure investment marked on the timeline as vertical bars. If ridership climbs and the injury rate per kilometer falls after a protected-lane investment, that is an argument no wethouder or alderman can talk their way around, because it answers the objection before it's raised — "but more people are riding now, of course there are more crashes." I have built approximations of this chart from Bicycle Account data for masterclasses, and it is the single most effective slide I own. Nothing on this Chicago site could produce it today, not because the visualization is hard, but because half the data doesn't exist yet.

**Interviewer:** And what would make you stop using a site like this after trying it once?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Two things, and I've watched both kill a tool's credibility with me before. First, if I catch even one number presented without its badge — if the mock obstruction heat or a proxy figure ever got copied onto a "findings" card without the caveat following it, I'd stop trusting the whole badge system immediately, because the entire value of that system is that it's applied without exception. Second — and this is more mundane — if the weekly refresh turned into a monthly refresh that quietly slipped to quarterly with no notice. I've had a client-side data feed do exactly that to me once, silently going stale for four months before I noticed a chart hadn't moved, and I used a dead number in a live presentation because of it. Once I catch a tool lying to me about its own freshness, I go back to building the spreadsheet myself, and I don't come back.

## Follow-up round

**Interviewer:** Let's unpack the missing-link tracker. What does the spreadsheet actually look like — columns, how a gap gets logged, how you decide a gap is a gap?

**Marloes van Dijk:** It's not glamorous, I promise you. Columns are: client, date, gap type, location description, what triggered the flag, and a confidence note — high, medium, guess. Gap type is where the pattern-recognition lives: ring-road crossing, industrial-zone gap, school-approach gap, and a fourth I added two years ago, "orphaned cycle track," meaning a good stretch of protected lane that just stops and dumps you into traffic. A gap becomes a gap when I get it from two sources that don't know about each other — the blank-map exercise I described, where two or three planners independently draw the same hole, plus my own eyeballing of the aerial photo. One source alone, I don't log it, I've been burned by "everyone says the crossing at X is bad" that turns out to be one loud resident.

Would I trust a Chicago version if OYL pre-populated candidate categories from the street and bikeway files? Cautiously, yes, and here's the honest distinction: I'd trust it as a *hypothesis generator*, not as the gap list itself. If your file flags "protected lane ends abruptly at an arterial crossing" algorithmically, that's a legitimate proxy for my orphaned-track category, and it would save me the aerial-photo squinting. But the value of my spreadsheet isn't the taxonomy — the categories are almost embarrassingly generic, any planner would invent the same four names. The value is the *convergence test*, that two humans who don't talk to each other draw the same hole. A machine-generated candidate list is one source. I'd still want the second source — a planner, a rider, someone who lives it — before I called it a gap in a client meeting.

**Interviewer:** And the rough proxy scorecard — what exactly goes into it, and how does it collide with OYL's ward table or ward safety index?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Four numbers usually, whatever's public: crash rate per capita — I'd rather have per-kilometer but I rarely get it for a small comparison town — infrastructure length as a share of total street length, some crude surface-quality proxy from open pavement condition data if the town has it, and, when I can get it, a modal-share figure from a household travel survey if one exists. No formal weighting — I say that out loud in the room, every time: "this is not Fietsbalans, there is no defended methodology behind these four numbers, I am adding them because I have nothing else and you asked for a number today." That sentence is doing real work — it's the caveat that keeps the scorecard from calcifying into a fact six months later when someone reuses my slide without me in the room.

Would I treat OYL's ward index as a ready-made version of mine? No — and this is an important distinction — I'd treat it as a *competing* one I'd have to reconcile, not adopt. Mine is town-versus-town, built for a client who wants "how do we compare to our neighbor." OYL's is ward-versus-ward inside one city, crashes per bikeway-mile, no exposure, which I already told you I distrust as a risk label. If a Chicago client showed me both my scorecard and OYL's index disagreeing on which ward looks worse, I would not silently pick one — I would say so in the room, "these two proxies disagree, here is why, neither has a denominator I trust, treat both as directional." That disagreement is actually useful information, it tells me where I need real data before anyone commits money.

**Interviewer:** You said you wouldn't forward the findings cards or the network map to anyone. What exactly breaks — is it the advocacy framing, the editorial-roster problem, or something about staking your name on it?

**Marloes van Dijk:** All three, honestly, but not equally. The network map I already explained — it's a roster, not cohesion data, and if I forwarded it to a client and they mistook it for an actual analysis the way I nearly did, that's my professional error landing on their desk with my name attached. That one is about accuracy risk to my reputation, plainly.

The findings cards are a different failure. It's not that they're wrong, several of them are honest, the dooring-undercount card especially. It's that they're written in a voice — curated, punchy, one stat plus one caveat — that's built to persuade a general public, not to survive a planner asking a follow-up question. If I forward a findings card to a client, I am implicitly vouching for it as evidence, and the moment they ask me "what's the confidence interval on hit-and-run reporting" or "what's the source methodology behind the KSI trend," I can't answer for someone else's advocacy copy. I can defend a SWOV fact sheet in that room because I know how SWOV builds it. I cannot defend a card built for a masterclass audience of the general public. So it's less "this is wrong" and more "I did not build this, I cannot stand behind it under questioning, and forwarding it without that standing-behind is just passing along someone else's homework with my signature on it."

**Interviewer:** The sponsorship-plus-outcome pairing you asked for — what artifact does that actually serve, and who's it for?

**Marloes van Dijk:** For me, honestly, before it's for anyone else. It's a due-diligence step, not a deliverable — the thing I'd run before I ever repeated an alderman's "safety record" claim to a client or in a masterclass. Concretely I'd want one line added next to each alderman's sponsorship count: crash trend and, if it existed, ridership trend for their ward, before and after the legislation date. That's not a chart I'd build for a wethouder-equivalent — it's the check I do quietly so that when someone asks me in a room "is Alderman So-and-so's safety record real," I don't repeat a number I haven't stress-tested.

Would I forward the pairing itself, once built, to a Chicago client? Only with heavy hedging, and only the pairing, never the raw sponsorship count alone — because the count without the outcome is exactly the "measures who talked about the thing, not who changed the thing" trap I described. If the outcome column existed and showed nothing changed, that's actually a useful, forwardable finding — "sponsored, no measurable effect" is a real sentence I'd put in a report. Without it, no, that whole layer stays in my own working file, not in anything I stake my name on.

**Interviewer:** On "menu money" — do you have a real Dutch or EU comparison point that could ground advice here, or is this genuinely outside your expertise?

**Marloes van Dijk:** [pause] Let me be honest instead of clever. We do have something adjacent — *wijkbudgetten*, neighborhood budgets, small discretionary pots some Dutch municipalities give district councils for local improvements, sometimes a bit of infrastructure money in there. But it's not a real comparison point, and I want to say clearly why: wijkbudgetten sit *underneath* a citywide or provincial network plan, they can't override it, a district can spend its pot on a nicer square but it cannot decide the cycling network stops five streets early. What you're describing with fifty aldermen sounds like the discretionary layer with no citywide plan sitting above it constraining it — and if that's actually true, I don't have a Dutch frame for that, because we don't let the small pot outrank the big plan.

So — would I decline to advise on it? Not decline outright, but I would say to a Chicago client exactly what I'm saying to you: "I can tell you how this would collide with network planning if it worked the way I fear it works, but I do not know your politics well enough to tell you how it actually gets negotiated, and I would need a local partner in that room, not me alone." I don't reason from a frame that doesn't map. That's how I got burned once advising on a French delegation's *contrat de ville* funding — different mechanism than I assumed, I gave a half-wrong answer, I don't repeat that.

**Interviewer:** Last one — if OYL added a bikeshare-trip-count layer, badged explicitly "proxy," to partially normalize the ward safety index, would that cross your line the same way mock obstructions do?

**Marloes van Dijk:** No — and I want to be precise about why, because it's not just "proxy good, mock bad" as a slogan. The bikeshare layer is a real, observed count of actual trips that actually happened. It is incomplete — it undercounts personal-bike riders, commuters who own a bike, whole categories of trips — and "proxy" is the exact right word for that incompleteness. But every number in it is a real event in the world. The mock obstruction layer is not incomplete, it's *invented* — nothing in it happened, it's a simulation of what obstructions might statistically look like. That's not a matter of degree, it's a different category of thing entirely, the same way I wouldn't confuse a partial crash count with a modeled crash count someone made up to fill a gap.

So yes, I'd use the bikeshare-badged layer, with the same caveat I gave you about the Fietsbalans-proxy scorecard: I'd say out loud, "this normalizes for bikeshare riders only, it says nothing about the far larger population riding their own bike, treat the resulting rate as a floor on exposure, not the true rate." That's a sentence I'm comfortable saying in front of a wethouder. "This heat pattern is synthetic but the color scheme matches the real data so don't worry about it" is not a sentence I will ever say in front of one, no matter how honest the badge is. The line isn't proxy versus mock as words on a legend — it's observed-but-partial versus never-happened-at-all. I can defend the first under questioning. I cannot defend the second, and I won't try.
