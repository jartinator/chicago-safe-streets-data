## Gap-probing round

**Interviewer:** And the exposure/ridership caveat specifically — does the site's continued refusal to normalize by ridership change how you'd use any of what we just walked through?

**Marloes van Dijk:** It changes everything, and it's the one thing that hasn't moved an inch across two versions of this site, which honestly surprises me a little less than it should — I keep expecting somebody to have found even a rough proxy in eight months, and nobody has. Every layer we just discussed inherits the same problem: the ward safety index, the ward one-pager built on top of it, the corridor findings, even the new BNA card, which is network *quality*, not volume, so it doesn't rescue the crash side either — I want to be precise that I don't conflate those two. Nothing here is a per-rider risk rate. I would say to a client today exactly what I said last time: treat every crash number as an incident log until a denominator exists, and I would still want *some* proxy — bikeshare counts, a handful of visible counters — before I'd call anything here a danger index rather than a raw tally with a ward attached to it.

**Interviewer:** What single absence, across everything you've now seen twice, costs you most?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Still exposure. Still ridership. I said it last time and it's more true now, not less, because the site has grown — more cards, more pages, more layers — around the same missing denominator, which means the gap is being built *around* rather than *closed*. A findings card, a ward one-pager, a national ranking score — all new surface area sitting on the same unnormalized foundation. More rooms added to a house with no foundation poured under half of it.

**Interviewer:** If OYL handed you one export a week, what would it contain now, given everything new?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Still the ward table CSV — unchanged answer, and I won't pretend the new material displaced it. ## Follow-up round

**Interviewer:** You said the agent-layer test — asking it the ward-safety-index question directly — is the thing you'd actually want to run before trusting it. Walk me through what a failing answer would look like to you, concretely.

**Marloes van Dijk:** A failing answer is any answer that states the number without restating its own limitation in the same breath. Concretely: if I asked "which ward is most dangerous for cyclists" and it answered with a ward name and the index number, full stop, that's a failure — it has laundered a *relative, unnormalized* score into an absolute claim, exactly the collapse I've been describing for two interviews now, except this time a machine did it on my behalf instead of a person who forgot the caveat. A passing answer says something closer to: "ward X has the highest relative safety index — this blends crash rate and bikeway-mile density, not ridership, so it isn't a true danger ranking, here's the ward table if you want the underlying numbers." The metadata header existing is necessary but not sufficient — headers can be technically present and still get summarized away by whatever's reading them. I'd want to actually run three or four adversarial questions like that before I let a client's staff loose on it unsupervised, the way I'd spot-check a junior planner's first few memos before trusting their judgment unread.

**Interviewer:** On the ward one-pager — if the limitation text were printed directly on the page rather than linked to the methodology page, would that fully satisfy you, or is there something else about a printed handout that still worries you?

**Marloes van Dijk:** It would satisfy the specific worry I named, yes, but there's a second one underneath it I didn't say out loud yet. Even with the caveat printed, a one-page handout compresses a safety index, a sponsorship record, and a spending proxy onto one sheet of paper an alderman's aide photocopies and staples to a memo. My worry isn't only "does the caveat survive," it's "does the *relationship* between those three numbers survive" — a reader skimming in an elevator, as you put it, will read sponsorship-plus-safety-index-plus-spending as one coherent verdict on the alderman, three numbers that agree, when in truth two of them are proxies and one has no denominator, and none of the three actually measure the same thing. Printing the caveat fixes the honesty of each number individually. It doesn't stop three uncertain numbers on one page from reading, at a glance, like one certain conclusion. That's a layout problem more than a data problem, and it's the kind of thing I'd only trust a native-English, native-Chicago-politics reader to judge properly — I'll flag it, but I hold this one loosely, it's closer to the edge of what I actually know.

**Interviewer:** You drew a line earlier between the gated obstruction page as a "schema demo for a negotiation" versus "a compromise because someone wanted the visual." How would you actually tell those apart from outside, without being told?

**Marloes van Dijk:** Honestly? Mostly I can't, from outside, and I should say that plainly rather than pretend I have a reliable test. The tell I'd look for: does the page's own text name a specific counterpart — a specific organization, a specific ask — or does it gesture vaguely at "a data-sharing conversation" with no named party and no visible timeline? A real negotiation has a name attached, even if the outcome is uncertain; a compromise dressed up as a negotiation tends to stay permanently vague about who's on the other side of the table, because there isn't really anyone there yet. If I had to bet today, I'd want to see that named counterpart before I fully believed the first explanation over the second. Absent that, I'd hold my verdict at "acceptable but unverified" rather than "acceptable," which is a real distinction for me, not just hedging for its own sake.

**Interviewer:** Last one. The PeopleForBikes score is crowdsourced-tier with an OSM-currency disclosure. Does knowing it's OSM-derived rather than officially surveyed change your appetite for it at all?

**Marloes van Dijk:** A little, yes, and I want to be precise about the direction. OSM-derived doesn't worry me the way "synthetic" worries me — OpenStreetMap is a real, if uneven, record of real infrastructure that real contributors mapped, so it sits in the same category as the bikeshare proxy I told you I'd use: observed-but-incomplete, not invented. What the currency disclosure actually buys me is knowing *when* to distrust a subscore — if a neighborhood's cycle infrastructure changed six months ago and nobody's re-traced it in OSM yet, the score there is stale, not wrong in principle, just out of date in that one place. That's a very different failure mode from the mock layer, and one I already know how to work around, the same way I work around my own missing-link tracker's confidence column — I'd just want the disclosure to be granular enough to tell me *which* wards or corridors are freshest, not just a single blanket currency date for the whole citywide score. A single date for a whole city hides exactly the kind of local staleness that bit me with the pothole spreadsheet.
