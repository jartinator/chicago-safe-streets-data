# Interview: chi-everyday-rider (PFB BNA proposal validation)

## 1. Context re-anchor

**Interviewer:** Quick check-in before we get into anything new — not about crashes this time. Tell me about the last time you had to figure out whether a street's bike setup was actually *good*, not whether something bad happened on it. How'd you land on an answer?

**chi-everyday-rider:** Oh — okay, this is different from what we usually talk about. Yeah, actually, last fall, when they redid California Ave near us, there was this whole stretch that got new green paint and little white posts, and everybody in the Avondale group was posting about it like "finally, a real bike lane." And I remember riding it with Mateo in the trailer the first week going, is this actually protected, or is this just paint with some plastic sticks that a truck's gonna run over by December? I didn't look anything up. I just rode it a bunch of times and watched — do cars still pull into it, does anybody actually stop for the posts, is there a spot where it just disappears and dumps you into traffic. By like the third week I had my own answer: it's fine until you get to the intersection by the auto shop, and then it's every man for himself. That's how I "figure out" if a street's any good — I ride it, more than once, and I watch what actually happens, not what the paint says is supposed to happen.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever compare notes with anyone else on that, or was it just your own laps?

**chi-everyday-rider:** Priya rides part of that stretch too, so yeah, a little — she texted something like "the new lane's cute till the auto shop, then it's nothing," which, exact same read as mine, so that made me trust my own read more, honestly. Two people noticing the same gap independently feels more solid to me than one official report would.

## 2. Product recap

**Interviewer:** Good, keep that in your head — the "is it really protected" question. Quick recap of what's on the site today, in plain terms: On Your Left has a regular street map with crash locations and what kind of bike lane is where, a separate subway-style map of the main routes, some finding cards with a stat and a warning label each, a ward table you can sort, a sources page listing where everything comes from, and a page that hands you 311, the lane-blocking-reports app, and your alderman's contact info. Every piece of data is tagged so you know if it's official, made-up-for-now, or somewhere in between. Nothing about how many people actually ride has been added yet — same as last time we talked.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Right, yeah, I remember most of that. Okay, so what's new?

## 3. Stimulus walkthrough

**Interviewer:** There's a new idea on the table — pulling in numbers from a different group entirely, not the city. A national bike advocacy organization scores basically every U.S. city's bike network once a year. Chicago just got scored an 11 out of 100.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Wait — eleven? Out of a hundred? Is that... is that for Avondale, or is that the whole city? Because if that's supposed to be grading my neighborhood I'm insulted, honestly, we just got that lane put in.

**Interviewer:** Whole city — every neighborhood averaged into one number.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Okay, that changes it a little, but not that much? Because my first thought either way is "well, that's it, don't bother," you know? Like if I saw that on a headline I'd think, biking here is basically hopeless, why am I even doing this every morning. It doesn't make me want to go do something about it, it makes me want to just drive Mateo. It's such a bad number that it stops feeling like information and starts feeling like a verdict.

**Interviewer:** For context — the average U.S. city they score gets a 36. And they say Chicago has 6,267 miles of stressful-to-bike streets and 1,834 miles that are calm enough to ride comfortably.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Okay, "average city gets a 36" actually helps a little, that at least tells me it's not just us being uniquely terrible, everybody's kind of bad, we're just worse-bad. That I could maybe use — like, "hey, we're not even average, other cities are doing better than us" is a sentence I could say to somebody. But the two mileage numbers, 6,267 versus 1,834 — I mean, I get the shape of it, more bad than good, but I couldn't tell you what to *do* with those specific numbers. It's not my street. It's not even close to my street. What would I even use it for — I guess if I wanted to tell my alderman "the whole city's bad, not just us," that's one sentence I could actually use. But it wouldn't tell me anything about whether to take California Ave tomorrow.

**Interviewer:** Before you'd trust that 11, is there anything you'd want to check?

**chi-everyday-rider:** Who's saying it, mostly. You said "advocacy organization" — advocacy for what, exactly? Like is this a bike company trying to sell more bikes, or is this legit? I'd want to know that before I forwarded it anywhere, because if my alderman's staffer looks it up and it turns out to be some random blog's number, that's on me, not just the site.

**Interviewer:** Next — the same group also scores every census block on how many people can get to things like the grocery store on calm, low-stress streets. If OYL published that by ward, it'd read like: "In Ward 35, X% of residents live somewhere with an easy bike route to a grocery store."

**chi-everyday-rider:** Ward 35 — is that... is that us? Okay, see, I genuinely don't know what ward I'm in, I know our alderman's name, I could not tell you the number if you paid me. So already I'm squinting going "wait, is that me?" If you told me "in Avondale" instead of "in Ward 35" I'd know instantly what you meant.

**Interviewer:** Say it's rephrased with your neighborhood name instead of the ward number — same idea: some percent of people near you can get to a grocery store on calm streets, some can't.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Okay, that's better, that's actually kind of close to a real question I have, because "can I get to the Jewel without white-knuckling it" is basically my life. But — is that a good number or a bad number? Like if it says 40%, is 40% good? I still want to know is that a "we're fine" or "we're bad" situation, because even though you're telling me it's not a danger ranking, in my head I'm still doing the ranking, I can't help it. I look at any percentage and my brain goes "is that a passing grade." So I don't know, I think I'd read it as a grade whether you meant it that way or not.

**Interviewer:** Fair — noted as a misread risk regardless of the framing. Next: the same source also grades individual streets, not just whole neighborhoods — how stressful each one is to actually ride, based on things like speed limit and how much traffic's on it, for every street, whether or not it has a bike lane at all.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Okay wait, *every* street? Not just the ones with lanes? — Now we're talking, that's actually the thing, that's literally what I was doing with my own eyes on California Ave last fall, except somebody already did it for the whole street? If that told me, in plain words, "this street is calm, this one's stressful, and by the way here's why — speed limit, how busy it is" — yeah, I'd use that. That's closer to red-yellow-green than anything you've shown me before. What would I check before trusting it — honestly, whether it matches what I already see. Like if it told me the auto-shop intersection on California is "fine" when Priya and I both watched cars blow through those posts, I'm not going to believe the number over my own eyes. I'd want to know how they even know this stuff — are people actually out there driving it, or is this off a map somewhere?

**Interviewer:** It's built off OpenStreetMap — a map that volunteers around the world edit and update themselves, not an official government survey.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Volunteers... so like Wikipedia, but for streets? Okay, that's a little bit "eh" for me — I mean I trust Wikipedia for, like, when a movie came out, I don't know if I'd trust it for whether a street's safe for my kid. What happens if nobody volunteered to map my block? Does it just not show up, or does it show up as fine because nobody flagged a problem? Because those are really different things and I bet it matters a lot which one it is.

**Interviewer:** Last one — the same score covers thousands of cities, so OYL could put Chicago next to a handful of others on the findings page — say, New York, LA, Philadelphia, Minneapolis, Seattle — same year, same method.

**chi-everyday-rider:** Huh. Okay, that one I don't really have a use for, if I'm honest. It's kind of interesting the way a stat on the news is interesting for like four seconds, but it's not my street, it's not even my city's problem I can do anything about — like, cool, Minneapolis is better than us, what am I gonna do, move? Maybe if I wanted one more line to throw at my alderman — "even Minneapolis beats us" — that's a sentence, sure. But honestly that's the one I'd scroll right past on my phone. It's trivia. Trivia with a point, maybe, but trivia.

## 4. Trust probes

**Interviewer:** A few direct questions. First — this whole thing is computed from that volunteer-edited map, by an outside advocacy group, updated once a year, and it's only as good as whatever's actually been mapped. Does knowing that change anything for you?

**chi-everyday-rider:** A little, yeah — mainly it makes me trust the "every street gets a stress score" one *less* than I did a second ago, because now I'm picturing some volunteer somewhere just... never got around to our block. And it makes the "once a year" part bug me too — the California Ave lane went in last fall, if their map hasn't caught up yet, is it just going to say that street's still bad because the posts weren't drawn in yet? I feel like I'd want the site to just tell me straight up, "heads up, if your street changed recently this might be behind," in normal words, not buried somewhere.

**Interviewer:** Second — Chicago's overall score is 11 out of 100. Does that number help you or hurt you?

**chi-everyday-rider:** Both, honestly, and I think that's the real answer, not a dodge. Hurts me if I'm just a person trying to decide whether to bike tomorrow, because it reads like "don't bother," and I could see somebody who's on the fence about biking at all just going, well, the internet says it's an 11, guess I'll drive. But it helps me if I'm the one trying to make a point to somebody — my alderman, or Danny when we argue about routes — because "we scored an 11, the average city scored a 36" is a real sentence with a real gap in it, that's ammo. So it's a good number to hand to somebody who's already trying to fix something, and a bad number to hand to somebody who's just trying to decide whether to get on a bike this morning. I don't think the site can have it both ways without saying which one it's for.

**Interviewer:** Third — that ward percentage is meant to describe access, like "can residents get where they need to go on calm streets," not a danger ranking. Your reaction to that distinction?

**chi-everyday-rider:** I hear you saying it's not a danger ranking, and I believe that's the intent, but I already told you — the second I see a percentage next to my neighborhood's name, I'm going to rank it in my head whether the label says to or not. That's just how I read any number. So I don't think you can fully stop that by wording it carefully. What might actually help is if right next to it there's something like "here's one thing that would move this number" — because a ranking with no next step just becomes a bad grade I feel bad about, but a ranking with a "here's why and here's what would fix it" attached at least gives me something to do besides feel bad.

**Interviewer:** Last one — say OYL's own map calls a lane "protected," but this new source says that same street is still high-stress because of the speed limit or the intersections. Which do you believe, and what should the site do about it?

**chi-everyday-rider:** I believe whichever one matches what I've actually ridden. That's not me being difficult, that's just — I have California Ave in my legs, I know where it falls apart. If OYL says "protected" and this other thing says "still stressful because of the intersection," and that intersection is the actual auto-shop one where Priya and I both watched it go bad — then honestly, the outside score just proved itself right to me, and I'd trust OYL less for calling it protected without the asterisk. What I don't want is to be shown both numbers side by side and told to go figure out which one's right myself — that's homework, and I already do that homework every morning on my actual bike. I want the site to have already done the arguing and just tell me "hey, this one's marked protected on our map but there's a rough intersection partway through, heads up" — one sentence, not two contradicting scores.

## 5. Forced choice

**Interviewer:** If you had to rank these four for your own actual use — the citywide 11/100 number, the neighborhood access percentage, the every-street stress rating, and the comparison to other cities — what order?

**chi-everyday-rider:** Street-level stress rating first, easy, because that's the closest thing to "is my route okay" that you've shown me all conversation, and it covers every street, not just the ones with lanes already. Second would be the neighborhood access percentage, only because it's at least about a place I recognize, even if I still don't fully know what to do with a percentage. Third, the citywide 11 out of 100 — not because it's useless, it's actually decent ammo for one sentence to somebody, but it's not going to change what I do tomorrow morning. Last, dead last, the other-cities comparison. That one's just trivia to me, I said it already — nice to know, does nothing for me.

**Interviewer:** Should OYL skip all four of these and put the effort somewhere else instead? If so, where?

**chi-everyday-rider:** Honestly — if you made me pick between "add this whole national scoring thing" and "just tell me how many bikes are actually using my street," I'd pick the second one every time, and you already know that's what I'd say, I've said it before. That per-rider number still doesn't exist and it bugs me way more than not having a national grade does. But I'll be fair — the every-street stress thing, if it's real and it's not off doing its own weird separate map, that one I actually would use, so I wouldn't necessarily kill the whole idea. I'd just want you to know that if it's a choice between polishing this outside score and finally telling me how busy my own street is, the second one wins, no contest.

## 6. Kill question

**Interviewer:** Last one. What single thing about adding this data, if done wrong, would make you stop trusting the site?

**chi-everyday-rider:** If it disagrees with what I can see with my own eyes and the site just shows me both numbers and shrugs — that's the one. Like if OYL's map says California Ave is protected, and this new score says it's still stressful, and I have to sit there going "well, which is it," that's not the site doing its job, that's the site handing me back the same guessing I already do every morning, except now with extra numbers on top of it. The whole reason I'd trust a site like this is so I don't have to be the one who reconciles conflicting information — I already am that person, in my actual life, riding the actual street. If OYL makes me do that math too, I stop trusting either number, and honestly probably the whole site along with it, because now it looks like even the people running it don't know which of their own facts is true.
