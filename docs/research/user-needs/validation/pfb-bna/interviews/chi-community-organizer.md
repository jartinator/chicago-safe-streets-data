# Interview: chi-community-organizer (PFB BNA validation)

## Transcript

**Interviewer:** Thanks for coming back, Andre. Quick re-anchor before I show you anything: same role as last time — coalition coordinator, task force seat — but this time I want to ask about *quality*, not crashes. Walk me through the last time you had to describe how *good* the bike network is out your way, not how dangerous it is. Different question, I know.

**Andre:** It is a different question, and it actually came up not that long ago. Task force got asked to write a letter of support for a state Safe Streets for All grant CDOT was putting together this spring — and one of the boilerplate categories on the state's form was something like "does the project area contain a connected low-stress network," check yes or no, describe. And I sat there with our CDOT liaison and I said, what does "connected low-stress network" even mean for us? Because we've got Chicago Avenue with paint for six blocks then nothing, we've got the trail access on the east edge that's great if you can get to it, and then everything else is just Cicero and Austin Boulevard with cars doing forty-five past a five-year-old on a bike. Is that "connected"? Is that "low-stress"? I ended up basically describing it block by block from memory and from our own field walk — we did one of those task force walks two summers back where we literally rode the proposed routes ourselves and wrote down where it felt bad — because there wasn't a number I could point to that answered the state's question for me. CDOT's engineer had his own facility-type map, protected versus painted versus nothing, and that helped, but "protected" on his map didn't always match "feels safe" on our walk — there's a stretch of buffered lane on Chicago Ave where the buffer's maybe eighteen inches and cars park right up against it, so it's protected on paper and it's a nightmare in person.

**Interviewer:** Who reads that description once you've written it, and what do they need to believe about it?

**Andre:** The state grant reviewer, who's never been to Austin and is reading forty of these letters — they need it to sound like it's backed by something more than my say-so, some methodology, not just "trust the community guy." And internally, our own coalition needs to believe I'm not making the network sound worse than it is to win a grant, or better than it is to make the task force's two years of work look successful. That's a real tension — I've had folks on our own side accuse me of both in different meetings.

**Interviewer:** Good — hold that thought. Let me tell you what OYL, the site we talked through last time, looks like today, in one breath, since you haven't seen it in a while. It's still the same read-only dashboard: crash map, bikeway network by facility grade, a findings page, a ward table with CSV export, an accountability page for alderman records and hearings. Since we last talked they've added coverage numbers — percent of streets with any bike infrastructure, percent protected — and split the routes onto their own schematic map. Nothing about network *quality* beyond what CDOT itself reports as installed. That's the gap I want to test today — a proposal to bring in a third-party quality measure. Sound right as a summary of where it stands?

**Andre:** That tracks with what I remember, yeah — CDOT's own facility type is still the only "how good is it" signal on there, which is exactly the gap I just described with the grant letter. Somebody else grading the network besides CDOT itself — I'm listening, but I've got questions before I trust it.

**Interviewer:** Let's go one piece at a time. First — a national nonprofit called PeopleForBikes runs something called a Bicycle Network Analysis every year, computed from OpenStreetMap data — that's the same open volunteer map that also feeds a lot of trail and route data OYL already uses. OYL is proposing to pull in their citywide scorecard: Chicago's overall network score is 11 out of 100, versus a 36 average across the roughly 3,000 U.S. cities they rate, and Chicago has 6,267 miles of high-stress streets against 1,834 miles low-stress. There'd be a trend line — 9, then 11, then 11.08 over the last three years. What's your first reaction, before I say anything else?

**Andre:** Eleven out of a hundred. My first reaction is honestly — that's a number I could use, and it's a number somebody could use *on* me, and I need to know which one happens first. It's national, it's not us grading ourselves, so it's got a kind of credibility a CDOT self-report doesn't have in a skeptical room — "don't take my word, take a whole outside organization's word." That's useful. But I've sat in enough downtown meetings to know exactly what happens to a number like eleven out of a hundred citywide — somebody uses it to say "the whole city's bad, nothing special about Austin, everybody's suffering equally," and now my specific ask about Chicago Avenue gets flattened into a citywide problem that's everybody's problem and therefore nobody's priority. I've watched that move before. A citywide number is a shield for somebody who doesn't want to name a neighborhood.

**Interviewer:** Noted. Second element — this one's ward-level, not citywide. Block-level access scores get joined up to ward boundaries — the same way the site already joins crash data to wards — and shown as something like: "in Ward 37, X percent of residents live on blocks with low-stress bike access to a grocery store." Not a 0-to-100 ranking, an access percentage. Immediate reaction?

**Andre:** Okay. Now say that number again, slower, because my first read — before you told me it wasn't a ranking — my first read of "access score" is still going to be somebody's grade, same as I told you last time about that 0-to-100 safety index. If you put "Ward 37: 22 percent low-stress access" on a slide, half my room hears "Ward 37 got a 22." So the framing has to survive being said out loud fast in a basement, not just survive sitting on a webpage where somebody can read the fine print.

But if it actually holds — if it's genuinely "only twenty-two percent of your neighbors can get to a grocery store without riding in traffic," that's not a grade, that's a fact about what's missing, and that is exactly the kind of thing I could stand up with. That's an access desert. I've said "we're a food desert and a bike desert, both, and they're the same streets" in a coalition meeting before, off the top of my head, no chart behind it. If this hands me the chart, I'd use it — Tuesday, next task force call, without waiting for you to convince me further.

Here's my real question though, and it's the one I always ask: who mapped this? Because this is built off the same volunteer map — OpenStreetMap — that fills in trail data and route data everywhere else on the site, and I already know from doing this work for twenty years that volunteer effort follows money and time and trust, same as 311 calls do. If nobody's out here on a bike with a GPS logging every curb cut and crossing in Austin the way somebody's doing it in Lincoln Park or Logan Square, then a "low access" score on my ward might not mean the streets are actually worse — it might mean nobody's mapped what we've got. And it cuts the other way too, which is the part that keeps me up — if the actual hazards, the door zones, the missing crossings, never got logged either, this thing could come back looking *better* than it is, and then somebody uses that to say "see, Austin's fine, don't need the money there." I have had that exact sentence said to me about 311 data. I will not be surprised to hear it said about this.

**Interviewer:** Hold onto that — I have a whole section coming back to exactly that concern. Third element: a segment-level cross-check. Where OYL's own map says a stretch of bikeway is "protected," but PeopleForBikes' stress data — which factors in speed limit, lane count, and intersections, not just the paint — rates that same stretch as high-stress, OYL would flag the disagreement. Reaction?

**Andre:** Now that — that I like on instinct, and I'll tell you why: that's basically what I already do by hand every time I don't trust a dooring flag in the crash data, I read the actual row myself instead of trusting the label. If the site's going to automatically catch "we called this protected but it's actually still scary," that's somebody else doing the fact-check I've been doing myself for years for free. That buffered stretch on Chicago Avenue I mentioned — eighteen inches of buffer, cars parked right against it — if this tool would've flagged that as "labeled protected, rated high-stress," I could've walked into that grant conversation with something better than "trust me, I rode it." That's a receipt I didn't have to build myself.

My worry with it is narrower: now I've got two grades that can disagree, and if I'm standing in front of CDOT's engineer and he asks why his own department's facility map says one thing and this outside score says another, I need the site to have already answered that, in plain language, before I get asked. If the answer is just "well, they disagree, pick one," I'm the one who looks unprepared in that room, not the website.

**Interviewer:** Fair. Fourth and last — a peer-city comparison, riding on that same citywide score. Chicago next to a handful of other big cities — say New York, LA, Philadelphia, Minneapolis, Seattle — same year, same methodology, on a findings page.

**Andre:** Honestly, of the four things you've shown me, this is the one I'd use least, and I want to be straight about that instead of being polite. It's not nothing — if Minneapolis is way ahead of us I could see quoting it once, the way I've quoted a North Side ward's 311 response time against ours, just to say "it's not that this can't be done, somebody's doing it." But I don't organize against Minneapolis. I organize against a specific alderman's office and a specific engineer's project list. A peer city comparison is a slide for a downtown press conference, not something I'm bringing to a block club. I'd rather you spend the effort on making the ward number bulletproof than on giving me five more cities to worry about.

**Interviewer:** Let's do the trust probes now, same four questions every time I run this. First: this whole thing is computed from OpenStreetMap, by a national advocacy group, updated once a year, and it's only as current as whatever volunteers have actually mapped. Does that change anything for you?

**Andre:** It changes everything about how loud the caveat needs to be, and it changes nothing about whether the underlying problem is real — because I already believe volunteer mapping runs thin on our side of town, I don't need convincing on that, I need it *disclosed*, prominently, every single place this number shows up. Same rule I gave you last time about the dooring undercount card — that thing works because it's stated in plain language right on the card, not buried in a methodology footnote. If "this score depends on what's been mapped, and mapping is uneven across neighborhoods" isn't sitting right next to every ward number in text a normal person reads, then this is a 311-undercount problem wearing a nicer outfit, and it took me years to teach my own coalition not to trust a sparse 311 map. I'm not doing that education twice.

**Interviewer:** Chicago scores 11 out of 100. Does that number help you or hurt you?

**Andre:** Depends entirely on who's got the mic when it gets said, same as I told you about the crash-dot map last time. In a room I control — my coalition, a CDOT sit-down where I set the frame first — eleven out of a hundred, next to the fact that it's a national methodology, not our numbers, is a hammer. Nobody in that room can say I'm exaggerating; a nonprofit in Colorado said it, not me. But I've been in the other room too, the one where somebody from the budget side hears "eleven out of a hundred citywide" and turns it into "well, it's bad everywhere, that's just Chicago" — which is the exact same move as "311 complaints are flat," it's a way of making a specific, fixable, neighborhood-level failure sound like an ambient condition nobody's responsible for. I'd want OYL to never let that number sit alone without the ward breakdown right next to it, because the citywide number by itself is too easy to hide behind.

**Interviewer:** The ward number, if it ships, is access-framed — "X percent of residents have low-stress access to groceries" — not a danger ranking. React to that specifically.

**Andre:** That's the right instinct, and I'll say that plainly because you got the safety-index thing wrong the first time we talked and I gave you grief about it — this is you actually listening. Access, not danger, means I can say "look how much is missing" instead of "look how scary this is," and missing is an investment argument, scary is a stay-away argument. That's the difference between a case for reparative investment and a case for writing us off, and it's not a small difference, it's the whole fight.

But I want to test it, not just accept it, because I've watched good framing survive the person who wrote it and die the minute somebody outside the room repeats it badly. If a reporter or somebody on the budget side takes "22 percent of Ward 37 residents have low-stress access to a grocery store" and shortens it in their own head to "Ward 37 scored 22" — which is one lazy sentence away — then the access framing didn't survive contact with the room, it just survived contact with the website copy. I'd want to actually stand in a church basement with this language once before anybody trusts that it holds. Words on a page surviving isn't the same as words surviving out loud.

**Interviewer:** Last trust probe: when OYL's own bikeway quality grade disagrees with the BNA stress rating for the same segment, which do you believe, and what should the site do about the disagreement?

**Andre:** I believe whichever one matches what I can verify with my own eyes, honestly — and for a stretch like that Chicago Avenue buffer, I trust the stress rating over CDOT's "protected" label, because I've ridden it and CDOT's label is wrong in practice even if it's technically correct on a spec sheet somewhere. But I know that's not a universal rule, that's just me having personally walked that one block. For a segment I haven't personally checked, I don't automatically believe the outside score over CDOT's either — because if the disagreement is really just "OSM hasn't caught up to a lane CDOT installed eight months ago," then the outside score is wrong, not CDOT, and I'd be handing an outdated number to an engineer who'd correctly tell me I'm behind, not him.

So what should the site do — don't silently pick a winner. Show both, say why they might disagree — could be OSM lag, could be the facility not living up to its label — and don't resolve it for me. Let me be the one who decides which one to trust for my specific block, the way I already decide which crash-record dooring flags to trust and which ones to read myself.

**Interviewer:** Forced choice now. Rank the four pieces — citywide score, ward access, segment cross-check, peer cities — for your own work. Then tell me: should OYL skip all of this and put the effort somewhere else instead? If so, where?

**Andre:** Ward access first, no contest — that's the one that turns into an argument I can use tomorrow, the access-desert framing, if it survives the way we just talked about. Segment cross-check second, because it does fact-checking work I currently do by hand and it protects me from getting caught flat-footed by CDOT's own engineer. Citywide score third — useful as a frame-setter, dangerous as a shield for somebody else, so it's a tool I'd use carefully, not one I'd lead with. Peer cities last, distant last — nice for a press release, not for a block club.

Should OYL skip all four and spend the time elsewhere? I'll be honest with you the way I was last time about the mock obstruction data — if you made me pick between finishing this and finally building the equity layer I asked for last round, the one that puts race and income data next to the crash and access maps, I'd take the equity layer every time, no contest. This access-desert idea is good, but it's still the same shape of problem I described last time with the plain crash map — a number about my neighborhood, floating with no explanation of *why* it's true, is exactly the kind of thing that gets weaponized. The equity layer is what makes "22 percent access" read as "decades of disinvestment" instead of "not enough demand out there." Without it, you've built a better microphone but you're still handing it to whoever grabs it first, and that's not always going to be me.

That said — if it's not either-or, if the team's got room to do both, don't let my answer read as "don't build this." Build it, but build the caveat as hard as the number, or don't ship it at all.

**Interviewer:** Last question, the kill question. What single thing about this integration, done wrong, would make you stop trusting the site?

**Andre:** If I ever catch Ward 37's access number sitting on a page — a table, an export, anywhere — with nothing next to it saying the score depends on what's actually been mapped, and I find out later that a chunk of our real bike infrastructure never got logged into OpenStreetMap so the score reads worse than the truth, or worse, some hazard never got logged so it reads better than the truth — either way, that's the same move as trusting a sparse 311 map, and I've spent years undoing that exact mistake in my own community. If OYL makes it without telling people, I'm not just done with that number, I'm done vouching for the site, the same as I told you last time about the fake obstruction layer. And if I ever hear that number get repeated downtown as "see, they don't even use their bikes over there" — game over, I pull it off every one-pager I've ever handed out with it on there, and I tell the task force to stop citing it too.
